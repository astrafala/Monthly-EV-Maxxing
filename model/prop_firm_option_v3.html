Part 1

The mathematics

Everything is built from two facts: a fair game stays fair however you play it, and money paid out has to come from somewhere. No prior knowledge of probability or trading is assumed.

Chapter 1

The contract you are buying

A proprietary trading firm ("prop firm") sells evaluations. You pay a fee and receive a simulated trading account. Nothing you trade reaches a real market and you can never lose more than the fee. If you meet the firm's targets without breaking its loss limits, you get a "funded" account, still simulated, on which the firm pays you a share of the profits you make. Those payments are called rewards or payouts.

We use FTMO's 100K 2-Step Challenge as the worked example throughout. Chapter 11 extends the analysis to 37 account types at 16 firms.

Words you need

  • Balance is the account value counting only closed trades. Equity also counts the open trade's floating profit or loss. FTMO's limits are checked on equity, in real time.
  • Maximum loss (the floor). If equity touches $90,000 the account is closed. We call the distance from the current balance down to the floor B (it starts at $10,000).
  • Daily loss limit. You may not lose more than $5,000 (5% of the starting balance) within one day, measured from the balance at midnight Prague time, open trades included.
  • Profit target. The distance from the current balance up to the target is called A.
  • Profit split. The share of withdrawn profit you keep: 80% at FTMO (90% through its scaling plan).
  • Stop-loss and take-profit. Orders that close a trade automatically at a loss of S dollars or a profit of T dollars. A trade with both attached is called a bracket. Its reward-to-risk ratio (RR) is T : S.

Why this is an option, and what kind

Your worst case is losing the fee. Your best case has no fixed ceiling. A payoff with a capped downside and an open upside is what finance calls an option, and the fee is its price. The question this document answers is whether the option is worth more than its price to someone who cannot predict the market. Chapters 2 to 6 show that it is, and by how much. Note what this does not say. The value does not come from "volatility" in general, and it does not depend on trading in any clever way. Chapter 5 shows exactly where it comes from.

Chapter 2

Probability from zero

2.1 Probability and expected value

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 saying how often something happens in the long run. A fair coin lands heads with probability ½.

The expected value (EV) of a bet is the average result per bet if you repeated it many times. You get it by multiplying each outcome by its probability and adding up.

Expected valueEV = p1 × outcome1 + p2 × outcome2 + …

Example. You win $10 on heads and lose $10 on tails: EV = ½ × 10 + ½ × (−10) = 0. You win $20 on heads and lose $10 on tails: EV = ½ × 20 − ½ × 10 = +$5.

Expected values add up. The EV of ten bets is the sum of their ten EVs, whether or not the bets are connected to each other. This one fact will matter a lot in Chapter 13 (hedging).

2.2 Fair games

A bet is fair when its EV is zero. A game made of fair bets has a remarkable property: no way of choosing your bets can change the fact that your expected total is zero. You can bet big or small, stop when ahead, double after losses. If every individual bet is fair, the expected result of the whole sequence is still zero. Mathematicians call such a process a martingale, and the formal version of this fact is the optional stopping theorem. Its conditions (bets of bounded size, and a stopping rule that ends the game in finite expected time) are all met by the rules in this document.

2.3 One trade is one bet

Suppose you buy EURUSD and attach a stop 50 pips below and a target 50 pips above. If you have no information about direction, the price is as likely to reach either level first. The bet wins $T or loses $S. What is the probability p of winning?

With no skill, the price process has no drift, so the trade is a fair bet before costs: its EV must be zero.

Win probability of a zero-skill bracketp × T − (1 − p) × S = 0   ⇒   p = S ÷ (S + T)

Chapter 3

Reaching a target before a floor

Each challenge phase asks one question: starting from your current balance, will you reach the target (A dollars above) before you hit the floor (B dollars below)? This is the oldest problem in probability, known as the gambler's ruin.

3.1 The answer in one line

Let P be the probability of reaching the target first. The game ends in one of two places: +A with probability P, or −B with probability 1 − P. Every trade is fair, so by Section 2.2 the expected final result must be zero, no matter how you traded:

Gambler's ruin, exactP × A − (1 − P) × B = 0   ⇒   P = B ÷ (A + B)

For FTMO Phase 1, A = B = $10,000, so P = 10,000 ÷ 20,000 = 50%. For Phase 2, A = $5,000 and B = $10,000, so P = 10,000 ÷ 15,000 = 66.7%.

3.2 Checking it by hand

Take a tiny version: steps of $1, target +2, floor −2. From 0 you need two net wins before two net losses. Call Px the chance of passing from x. Then P2 = 1, P−2 = 0, and every middle point is the average of its neighbours: Px = ½Px+1 + ½Px−1. The only numbers that are "the average of their neighbours" lie on a straight line, so Px = (x + 2) ÷ 4 and P0 = ½, exactly as the formula says.

3.3 The value of the phase is a straight line

The same argument works from any balance x between the floor and the target:

Pass probability from balance xP(x) = (x + B) ÷ (A + B)    (x measured from the starting balance)

At −$9,900 in Phase 1, with only $100 left above the floor, your chance is 100 ÷ 20,000 = 0.5%. You can only risk $100 there, and the formula already accounts for that: it is the room left above the floor that sets the probability.

3.4 What does not change the answer

Look at what the formula does not contain: no bet size, no reward-to-risk ratio, no number of trades, no volatility. As long as every trade is fair and the account ends exactly on the target or exactly on the floor, the pass probability is B ÷ (A + B). Sizing up after losses, sizing down near the floor, 1 : 1 versus 1 : 3: none of it moves the number. Sizing only changes how many trades it takes, and Chapter 7 shows that the number of trades is what you pay for in costs.

This also means the earlier documents' central claim, that the challenge pays off because "variance is rewarded" (Jensen's inequality), is the wrong explanation. With no time limit, more variance does not raise the pass probability at all. Variance only helps when there is a deadline.

3.5 What does change it: overshoot

The formula assumes the account lands exactly on a barrier. If your last winning trade carries you past the target, the extra profit is wasted, and the pass probability falls. If your last losing trade carries you past the floor (a price gap through your stop), the firm absorbs the extra loss, and the pass probability rises.

Gambler's ruin with overshootP = (B + Ofloor) ÷ (A + Otarget + B)

Example. The earlier documents traded a fixed ±$4,800 at 1 : 1. Two wins reach only +$9,600, which is short of the $10,000 target, so a third net win is needed and the account effectively has to reach +$14,400. Then P ≈ 10,000 ÷ (14,400 + 10,000) = 41% instead of 50%. Choosing a fixed size without looking at the target costs nine percentage points.

Rule from Chapter 3

Size every trade so that a win lands exactly on the target and a loss lands no lower than the floor. Under that rule the pass probability is fixed at B ÷ (A + B), and the only thing left to optimise is cost.
Chapter 4

The challenge, step by step

The cheapest way to land exactly on a barrier is to use as few trades as possible. Within FTMO's $5,000 daily loss limit that means risking up to $5,000 per trade and aiming the take-profit straight at the target. Mathematicians call this bold play. Here is the complete tree.

So P = ¼ + ½P, which gives P = ½. It is the same answer, as Chapter 3 promised. Bold play just gets there in at most two trades.

4.3 Details the tree hides

  • Minimum trading days. If you pass on day 1 or 2, you still need 4 trading days. Place a tiny trade (for example 0.01 lots with a 5-pip stop) on the remaining days. It changes nothing measurable.
  • The daily limit includes open losses. Keep each stop a little under $5,000 (the real-data test used $4,850) so spread and slippage cannot breach the limit.
  • Phases are independent. Phase 2 starts again from $100,000, so its 2/3 does not depend on how Phase 1 went.
  • This is the theoretical optimum, not the Part 2 plan. Risking the full daily limit on one trade aimed at the target is what FTMO's conduct rules call overexposure or a one-sided bet (Chapter 17). Part 2 keeps risk at 1.5% per trade and gets its speed from the instrument and the stop size instead (Chapters 8–10).
Chapter 5

What a funded account is worth

Once funded, there is no target. You trade, withdraw profits every 14 days, keep 80%, and the account continues until equity hits the $90,000 floor. How much do you expect to collect in total?

5.1 An accounting identity

Follow the money. Every dollar withdrawn from the account came either from trading profit or from the balance going down. Over the whole life of the account:

Funded-account identity (always true)Total withdrawn = Total trading profit + (Starting balance − Final balance)

The account ends when the balance reaches the floor, so Starting − Final = $10,000. With zero skill, total trading profit is a sum of fair bets, whose expected value is zero (Section 2.2; the stopping conditions hold because the account is reset to $100,000 at every payout, which guarantees it ends in finite expected time). Taking expectations:

Expected payout of a funded accountE[total withdrawn] = 0 + $10,000 = $10,000   ⇒   you receive 80% × $10,000 = $8,000 (minus costs)

In words: the firm's expected payout equals the loss allowance it gives you. You keep 80% of every dollar you withdraw, and the firm pays every dollar of loss down to the floor. In a fair game, the dollars won balance the dollars lost, and the losses stop at exactly $10,000. This is the true source of the option's value. It does not depend on how you trade.

5.3 Why the earlier "expected maximum = $10,000" claim is false

The earlier documents argued that a fair walk with a floor $10,000 below reaches an expected maximum of $10,000. The chance that it ever climbs m dollars before the floor is 10,000 ÷ (10,000 + m), which falls so slowly that the expected maximum is infinite. The correct reason the payout averages $10,000 is the identity in 5.1, which depends on withdrawing profits. It also shows that their simulated averages of $10,271 (which would mean $12,839 withdrawn) and 13.6% × $22,000 (which would mean $8,985 to you per funded account) are impossible with zero skill.

5.4 The refund and the first payout

The €540 fee comes back with your first payout, so it pays to make the first payout very likely. Aim first for a small profit h. The chance of reaching +h before the floor is 10,000 ÷ (10,000 + h). With h = $500 that is 95.2%. Once you reach +$500, stop trading until the payout date, collect $400 plus the refund, then continue normally. The refund adds about 0.95 × $632 ≈ $600 to the funded value, for a total of about $8,600.

6.2 If it is this good, why does the firm survive?

On a perfect zero-skill trader, FTMO expects to pay out about $2,870 for every $632 fee. Firms survive for three reasons.

  • Most customers are far worse than random. Industry estimates put real Phase 1 pass rates at 10–15%, against 46–50% for a low-cost coin flip. The difference is mostly trading costs from many small trades with tight stops (Chapter 7), plus abandoning live accounts.
  • Rules remove convexity where it is cheap to remove: trailing drawdowns, consistency rules, payout caps (Chapter 11).
  • Payouts are discretionary, and the conduct rules target exactly the fastest version of the strategy (Chapter 16).
Chapter 7

Trading costs

Every trade pays the spread (the gap between buying and selling price), a commission, and sometimes slippage (being filled worse than your stop price). Costs are the only thing in this model that turns fair bets into losing ones.

7.1 Measuring cost per trade

For EURUSD on FTMO, a realistic all-in cost is about 0.75 pip per round trip: roughly 0.15 pip of raw spread, 0.5 pip of commission (about $5 per standard lot) and 0.1 pip of average slippage. What matters is the cost relative to the stop. We call this ratio κ (kappa):

Cost ratioκ = cost in pips ÷ stop distance in pips     e.g. 0.75 ÷ 55 ≈ 1.4%

A trade risking $1,000 with a 55-pip stop costs about $14. The same $1,000 risk with a 10-pip stop needs 5.5 times the position size, so it costs about $75 (κ = 7.5%).

7.2 Costs as a drift

A cost of c dollars per trade turns the fair bet into one with EV = −c. The pass formula becomes, approximately:

Pass probability with costsP ≈ (B − expected total cost) ÷ (A + B)

7.3 Why the number of trades is what you pay for

With fair trades of size L (risk L, target L), the expected number of trades to finish a phase is A × B ÷ L2. Each costs about κ × L, so:

Expected total cost of a phaseCost ≈ κ × L × (A × B ÷ L2) = κ × A × B ÷ L

7.4 How to keep costs low

  • Wide stops are cheap but slow. A stop of about one day's range keeps κ near 1–1.5% on EURUSD, but each trade then lasts days. Chapter 11 shows how to trade cost against time.
  • Liquid instruments and hours. EURUSD during the London and New York sessions has the tightest spreads.
  • Avoid scheduled news. Rate decisions, US payrolls and CPI cause slippage on stops.
  • Swap. Holding overnight earns or pays a small interest charge. With ~1–2 day trades it is minor, but check your platform's swap table.

With κ ≈ 1.4% applied to every trade, the simulated EV of the bold plan falls from +$2,272 to +$2,081 (100,000 simulated attempts).

Chapter 8

Time: how long an attempt takes

Expected value per attempt is only half the story. If one plan earns $1,400 per attempt and needs 86 weeks, and another earns $1,400 and needs 3 weeks, the second is worth almost thirty times more per month. This chapter derives how long each phase takes and which choices make it faster.

8.1 How many trades a phase needs

Take fair trades that risk L and aim for k × L (reward-to-risk 1 : k). Each trade adds a random amount with mean zero and variance k × L2. A second fair-game identity (Wald's) says the expected squared final result equals the expected number of trades times the variance per trade. The final result is +A with probability B ÷ (A + B) and −B otherwise, so its expected square is A × B.

Expected number of trades in a phaseN = A × B ÷ (k × L2)

FTMO Phase 1 (A = B = $10,000) with 1% risk and 1 : 1 needs about 100 trades. With 1.5% risk and 1 : 3 it needs about 10,000 × 10,000 ÷ (3 × 1,5002) ≈ 15 trades.

8.2 How long one trade takes

A bracket closes when price moves a distance s against you or k × s in your favour. If the price moves with volatility σ per hour (its typical one-hour move), the same identity applied to price gives:

Expected duration of one bracketTtrade ≈ k × s2 ÷ σ2    hours

A stop of one hourly move (s = σ) at 1 : 1 takes about one active hour. A stop of three hourly moves takes about nine.

8.3 Time and cost of a whole phase

Multiply the two. The reward-to-risk ratio k cancels:

Phase time and phase costTime ≈ A × B × s2 ÷ (L2 × σ2)      Cost ≈ A × B × c ÷ (k × L × s)

Here c is the round-trip cost as a fraction of price. These two lines contain every lever:

8.4 The speed–cost trade-off

A tighter stop finishes the phase sooner but costs more per dollar risked. If the expected value per attempt falls roughly in a straight line with cost, EV ≈ EV0 − β / s, and time grows like s2, then EV per unit time is (EV0 − β/s) ÷ s2. Setting its derivative to zero gives s = 1.5 β ÷ EV0, where EV is one third of EV0.

The surprising rule

When time is what matters, the best stop is tight enough that trading costs eat about two thirds of the no-cost expected value. Fixed waiting times (minimum days, reviews, the 14-day payout calendar) push the optimum back towards wider stops, because once those waits dominate, more speed buys little. Chapter 10 finds the real optimum by simulation.

8.5 Fixed waits you cannot trade away

  • Minimum trading days: 4 per phase at FTMO, 3 at FundingPips and The5ers, 5 at FundedNext.
  • Reviews: about 1 day between phases and 1–3 days for identity checks and the contract before funding.
  • The payout calendar: FTMO's first payout comes 14 days after your first funded trade, then every 14 days. FundingPips offers weekly payouts at a 60% split. FundedNext's first cycle is 21 days. Crypto firms such as Breakout and HyroTrader pay on demand.

These waits set a floor. Even the fastest pass cannot produce an FTMO payout much sooner than about 25 days after purchase: 4 + 4 minimum trading days, about 4 days of reviews, then 14 days to the first payout date.

9.2 Crypto: trading 24/7 does not help

Bitcoin moves about six times as much as EURUSD in a week, partly because it trades on weekends. But on prop platforms it costs fifteen to twenty times as much per trade, because crypto carries percentage commissions (0.0325% per side at FTMO, 0.04% at Breakout, 0.055% taker at Bybit). Its efficiency ends up between a half and a tenth of the alternatives. Chapter 10 confirms it: the best crypto set-up found earns about a fifth of the Nasdaq plan per month.

9.3 What leverage actually does

Leverage does not make trades resolve faster. Time depends on how far price must travel (the stop distance) compared with how fast it moves. Leverage only limits position size, and so sets the tightest stop you can use for a given risk:

Tightest stop allowed by leverage λsmin = risk per trade ÷ (λ × account size)     1.5% risk at 1:50 → 0.03%; at 1:3.3 → 0.45%

For indices, metals and currencies the limit is far below any sensible stop. For crypto at FTMO (1:3.3), 0.45% is just above one hourly Bitcoin move, so leverage would block the fastest crypto stops. Costs make those stops unattractive anyway.

9.4 Overnight financing

Positions held through the daily rollover pay (or occasionally earn) financing on their full size. With tight stops the position is large, so even one night can cost several percent of the risk on index and gold CFDs. Fast plans close most trades within hours, which keeps this small. The simulations charge it on every rollover crossed.

9.5 Futures

Futures are the cheapest instrument of all, but futures prop firms use trailing drawdowns, small accounts ($2,000 allowance on 50K), payout ladders and consistency rules (Chapter 11). Their value per account is small, though attempts are quick and some firms allow many accounts at once.

Chapter 11

Other firms and other rule types

Version 3 note

This chapter is version 1's firm comparison. Chapters 17–21 redo it on price paths and find a better plan for every major firm (Chapter 20). Where the figures differ, Chapters 17–21 hold.

Every challenge can be valued with the same two tools: a pass probability for each phase, and the identity for the funded account. What changes between firms is the shape of the floor and the payout rules.

11.1 Static floor

FTMO 2-Step and most forex/CFD 2-Steps work as in Chapters 3–5: P = B ÷ (A + B) per phase, funded value ≈ split × B.

11.2 End-of-day (EOD) trailing drawdown

The floor rises whenever a day closes at a new high, and stays there. If you can pass in a single day, the floor never moves and you get the static answer. Firms add consistency rules ("your best day may not exceed X% of total profit") to force several profitable days, each of which lifts the floor.

11.3 Intraday trailing drawdown

The floor follows your highest live equity, including unrealised profit. With prices that move continuously, the best possible chance of reaching A before a drawdown of D from the peak is:

Intraday trailing, any strategyP = e−A/D    (for driftless prices, the peak reached before a drawdown of D is exponentially distributed with mean D)

11.4 Funded accounts with locks and caps

Futures firms typically start the funded floor at the same trailing distance, then "lock" it at the starting balance plus about $100 once you are far enough ahead. They also cap payouts (for example Apex closes the account after six payouts). The identity still holds, so the funded value is split × (expected start minus final balance). Locks and caps reduce it, typically to around $1,500 on a $2,000 allowance, and to about $700 when the trail is intraday.

11.5 The ranking

We valued 37 account types at 16 firms the same way: zero skill, best sizing for each rule set, 1% trading costs, payouts honoured. The full table is in Appendix B.

  • Static 10% 2-Steps (FundedNext, FundingPips, The5ers, FTMO): +$2,000 to +$2,500 per attempt.
  • FTMO 1-Step ranks first (+$3,465): its trailing floor resets with each payout and the split is 90%.
  • Futures evaluations are cheap and return the most per dollar of fee, but each is worth only $300–$550.
  • Intraday trailing accounts and 3-step programmes are worth close to nothing, or less.
  • Instant-funding values are upper estimates: their payout caps and consistency rules were not fully modelled.

12.2 The Kelly criterion, done correctly

The Kelly criterion picks the bet size that makes your wealth grow fastest over time. For a bet with mean gain μ and variance σ2 per unit staked, the growth-optimal stake is roughly μ ÷ σ2 of your wealth. For a fixed-size bet such as one challenge, this inverts: the bankroll at which buying one attempt is growth-optimal is about σ2 ÷ μ.

Bankroll for one attempt at full KellyW ≈ σ2 ÷ μ     exact log-growth on the real-data outcomes: ≈ $4,100 (bold) and ≈ $6,000 (slow 1% plan)

The earlier documents computed μ ÷ σ2 = 1.4 × 10−5, treated it as a fraction of wealth, and concluded you need $40 million. The units are wrong: μ ÷ σ2 is "per dollar", not a percentage. Half-Kelly (about $8,000–12,000) is a sensible minimum for someone who wants smaller swings.

Chapter 13

Hedging

The idea is to take opposite positions in two accounts, at the same firm, at two firms, or against a personal brokerage account, so that one account's loss is the other's gain.

13.1 Hedging cannot create value

Expected values add (Section 2.1). The expected total of several accounts is the sum of their individual EVs, however their results are linked. If each account is worth +$2,235, a hedged pair is worth exactly +$4,470. If each were worth −$100, hedging would lock in −$200. A hedge changes only the spread of outcomes.

13.2 The values are straight lines, so a hedge can be exact

With a static floor, the value of each stage is a straight line in its own balance (Section 3.3):

Stage (no costs)Value at startValue per $1 of balance (slope)
Funded account$8,6020.80
Phase 2$5,7358,602 ÷ 15,000 = 0.573
Phase 1$2,8675,735 ÷ 20,000 = 0.287

Holding an opposite position equal to the slope makes the total value constant. For example, two Phase 1 accounts with opposite positions (equal target and floor distances) always produce exactly one pass, and a personal account holding 0.287× the opposite exposure locks in $2,867 − $632 per attempt. Mathematically, hedging turns the lottery of Chapter 6 into a near-certain cash flow.

13.3 Why it does not work in practice

  • It is prohibited. FTMO's forbidden-practices page bans opposite positions across accounts, explicitly including "accounts held with various operators/providers", with forfeiture of rewards and termination of all agreements. Most firms have equivalent clauses.
  • It cannot protect against a refused payout. Against your own brokerage account, the paths where the prop account wins are exactly the paths where your real money lost. If that payout is refused, the loss is real, up to $8,000 per funded account, where the unhedged loss would only have been the fee.

Hedging is therefore covered here only to explain the mathematics. It is not part of the execution plan.

Chapter 16

What the mathematics cannot tell you

Every number so far assumes the firm pays what it owes. That assumption is the weakest link, and no model can price it.

  • Payouts are discretionary. Funded accounts are simulated, and "rewards" are paid under terms that let the firm refuse them for practices it considers non-genuine.
  • Conduct rules. FTMO lists "overleveraging, overexposure, one-sided bets, account rolling" and hedging across accounts as forbidden, and recommends risking 1–1.5% per trade. The bold plan of Chapter 4 matches the prohibited pattern.
  • Firm failure. About 80–100 prop firms closed in 2024–25. True Forex Funds closed owing about $1.2 million; The Funded Trader admitted to more than $2 million in denied payouts; MyForexFunds was shut by a CFTC action in 2023.
  • Rule changes. Fees, splits and limits change often. Each change moves the numbers in this document.
  • Behaviour. The maths assumes you follow the plan exactly through long losing runs. Skipping trades, sizing up to "win it back" or quitting live accounts all lower the result.
  • Model limits. The real-data test covers 2.8 years of one currency pair. A different period could show more or less mean reversion. Costs differ between platforms.
Break-even payout refusal rate (FTMO 2-Step, 1% costs)EV ≥ 0 as long as fewer than about 77% of owed payouts are refused

That looks comfortable, but refusals are not independent coin flips. A refusal usually comes with the account closed and the person barred, which ends all future attempts. The realistic protection is to trade in a way that does not breach the terms at all. That is the purpose of Part 2.

17.3 Four traps found on the way

Each of these made a simulation look better than reality. All four are fixed in every figure of version 3, and each one is also a trap for anyone writing their own test.

  1. Skipping the entry bar. Checking the stop and target only from the next hour onwards ignores what the price did in the first hour. It handed a zero-skill 1 : 3 trade a fake +0.14 R per trade.
  2. Checking the price too rarely. Sampling the path 12 times an hour misses stop touches between samples. The nearer level (the stop) is missed more often, which faked +0.11 R per trade.
  3. Overshooting the target with several positions. When one position hits the phase target, the others are still open; their profit beyond the target is wasted. Without a cap on the combined target, the pass rate with four positions fell from 49.6% to 43%, while the average balance at a pass rose to +$13,665 (Chapter 18).
  4. A take-profit equal to the distance left. Costs are charged on the whole position, so a "win" of the last $40 can still lose money and the account never lands. The target must be the distance left plus the trade's cost (already a rule in version 2).

Chapter 18 · new in version 3

Many positions at once

The idea: instead of one trade at a time on one instrument, open several at once (Nasdaq and S&P 500, or many pairs), so the account reaches its target or floor sooner. This chapter works out what that can and cannot do, then tests every variant on price paths.

18.1 Three facts

  1. The pass chance does not change. A fair game stays fair however many bets run at once, so a phase still passes with probability B ÷ (A + B). That holds only if profit beyond the target is not wasted (18.3).
  2. Correlated positions in the same direction are one bigger bet. Two positions risking L1 and L2 on instruments with correlation ρ carry the risk of a single position of √(L12 + L22 + 2ρL1L2). Nasdaq and S&P 500 move together with ρ ≈ 0.9, so 1.5% on each is about 2.9% on one idea. That is twice FTMO's 1–1.5% guidance on what is really one trade. It also costs more than a single 2.9% Nasdaq position, because the S&P CFD costs about twice as much per unit of movement (Chapter 9).
  3. Independent positions add speed, but the calendar does not move. Two independent positions double the rate at which the balance moves, so trading time halves. But an attempt also contains minimum trading days, account reviews and payout dates, which no amount of trading shortens.

18.3 The overshoot trap

With several positions open, the target can be crossed by more than one of them. Profit beyond the target is pass probability thrown away: by optional stopping, P(pass) = (B + overshoot at the floor) ÷ (A + B + overshoot at the target + overshoot at the floor). Four uncapped positions (no costs) passed Phase 1 only 43% of the time, against the theoretical 49.6%, because the average balance at a pass was +$13,665 rather than +$10,000. Capping the combined target of all open positions at the distance left, and closing everything once equity reaches the target, restored 49.9%.

Verdict

One position at a time, on the cheapest instrument per unit of movement, at the top of the firm's guidance. More positions help only if a second instrument is as cheap as the Nasdaq and independent of it, and none is. The way to grow income is more accounts, not more positions per account (Chapter 21).

19.4 Why 1 : 5 beats 1 : 3

Trades per phase fall in proportion to the reward-to-risk ratio k (A × B ÷ k L2), and so does the spread paid. Time per trade rises in proportion to k, so the time per phase barely changes. The gain stops around 1 : 5 to 1 : 8, because near a target most trades are capped at the distance left anyway. On the real 2024–26 path the best was 1 : 4 to 1 : 5. Version 3 uses 1 : 5.

The price of 1 : 5 is psychological. Only about one trade in six wins. A run of ten losses in a row has a probability of about (5/6)10 ≈ 16% at any given point and will happen many times a year. Nothing is wrong when it does.

19.5 Funded accounts: run +10% cycles

With zero edge, the total paid out by a funded account averages the split times its loss allowance, whatever the payout policy (Chapter 5). What the policy changes is time. A small first target (+$1,000) reaches the first payout safely, but then the account sits idle for days waiting for the date. Aiming for +10% in every cycle finishes accounts sooner and recycles the slot into a new attempt. At FTMO (1 : 3): $3,634 per month with +10% cycles against $3,272 with +$1,000 first.

Pick one simple rule, write it down, apply it identically on every account, and plan on the zero-edge figures. If after several hundred trades your rule's full-size win rate at 1 : 5 is clearly above 1 in 6 after costs, that is the first evidence of an edge; until then there is none.

The whole document in six lines

A zero-skill trade is a fair bet, so a phase passes with probability B ÷ (A + B). A funded account pays out, on average, the split times its loss allowance. Time is set by the instrument's cost per unit of movement, the stop, the reward-to-risk ratio and the firm's calendar. The best plan found is one Nasdaq position at a time, stop 0.75 hourly moves, 1 : 5, risk 1.5%, funded cycles of +10%. That gives about $4,000 per month per 100K slot and, across the caps of seven CFD firms, about $102,400 per month per person in theory. More positions per account do not help; more accounts do. The firms' discretion is the one thing the maths cannot price.

D.1 Method in one paragraph

Synthetic paths: 12 years of hourly bars, zero-drift random walk at the Nasdaq's hourly volatility (0.27%), exact Brownian-bridge extremes, weekends closed. Costs: 0.0064% of notional per round trip (US100 CFD), 0.0027% (micro futures), plus financing. 24,000 synthetic and 8,000 real-path attempts per firm; portfolios from 2,000 twelve-month histories per firm.
