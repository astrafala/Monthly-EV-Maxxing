Chapter 7

What a funded account pays

On the funded account the plan trades each payout cycle to a profit target \(X\) above the starting balance, stops, and on the first allowed day requests everything above the start, or the firm's cap where it has one (GFT, The5ers, Topstep, Apex), the rest staying in the account. The request is then pending until the firm processes it (6.6). The balance is back at the start (plus any amount above a cap), the static floor is still \(D\) below the start, and the next cycle begins. If a payout date comes before the target, the plan requests whatever profit there is (where the firm's rules allow) and starts a new cycle; the cycle formulas below describe the simpler policy of paying only at the target, and the engine simulates the plan's. The account ends in a breach at the floor under the rule of 5.4 (or, at Apex, after a fixed number of payouts; at Blue Guardian at its second Guardian Shield; at GFT and Alpha Capital, left just above its floor; or, rarely, closed within cents of its floor, 5.4).

7.1 The withdrawal identity, with its conditions

Take one cycle. Its balance (relative to the start) is a stopped sum of trades, bounded between \(-D\) and \(X\) plus one win, and each trade has conditional mean minus its own cost, so optional stopping applies to it (2.5). Let cycle \(i\) end with balance \(Z_i\) after costs \(K_i\), and let \(Q_i \ge 0\) be any profit removed from the account without being paid to the trader or counted as a cost (a firm's deduction of profit above a day cap, profit voided by a rolling cap, a win voided for its holding time, a pending payout cancelled by a breach before it is processed). Then \(\mathbb{E}[Z_i] = -\mathbb{E}[K_i] - \mathbb{E}[Q_i]\). The account's total withdrawal is \(W = \sum_i Z_i\,\mathbf{1}\{\text{cycle } i \text{ paid}\}\) and it ends with balance \(X_{\text{end}}\) in its last cycle. Adding the cycles (the number of cycles has finite mean because each fails with a probability bounded away from zero):

The withdrawal identity, general form $$\mathbb{E}[W] = -\,\mathbb{E}[X_{\text{end}}] - \mathbb{E}[\text{total funded cost}] - \mathbb{E}[Q].$$

External credits (The5ers' hub credit, refunds) are paid outside the account and are added separately. Under the plan \(Q\) is small but not zero. Wins are capped at what is left of GFT's daily cap and of Maven's ceiling and 30-day window, and no position is open through a gap, so nothing is deducted there (in practice a take-profit filled a few points better than its price at an exact cap gives up those few points). But a GFT funded win held under a minute is voided ([[GFT_VOID]]), and a payout request still pending when the account breaches is cancelled, its amount lost with the account ([[CANCELLED]]); version 9 wrote \(Q = 0\) (review of version 9, findings 1 and 8). Under the terminal rule of 5.4 every account ends at the floor, \(X_\text{end} = -D\) (at the firms that allow its last trade), and the identity becomes \(\mathbb{E}[W] = D - \mathbb{E}[\text{cost}]\), the form version 6 stated without its conditions. It fails when the account can end elsewhere: a daily-limit breach part-way down, a closure after a fixed number of payouts (Apex), a second Guardian Shield (Blue Guardian), an account closed within cents of its floor (5.4), an account left just above its floor (GFT and Alpha Capital, 5.4), or a floor that moves (Topstep, Apex); for those accounts only the engine's figures are used. The cycle-by-cycle argument is needed because the balance plus withdrawals is not bounded.

7.2 Cycle by cycle

Each cycle is a fresh run of the chain of 5.5 from 0 to its target \(X_i\) before the floor, with success chance \(q_i\). Cycles continue until one fails, so cycle \(j\) is paid with probability \(\prod_{i\le j} q_i\). A win may pass the target (the minimum target distance of 4.1), so a successful cycle pays its balance at success, not exactly \(X_i\): write \(m_i = \mathbb{E}[\text{balance at success of cycle } i \mid \text{it succeeds}]\), which the chain gives as \(\mathbb{E}[X_\text{end}\mid\text{pass}]\) of 5.5. With a first cycle \((X_1, q_1, m_1)\) and later ones \((X, q, m)\), in the chain's model of independent cycles that each restart from zero:

Funded cash, cycle form $$\mathbb{E}[C] = \alpha\left(m_1 q_1 + m\,\frac{q_1\,q}{1-q}\right) + R\cdot q_1 q^{\,r-1},\qquad m_i = \text{the mean amount withdrawn at a success},$$

where \(\alpha\) and \(R\) are net of any share the firm takes from every payout (\(\alpha\) = split \(\times\) (1 − fee), and the refund less the same share: 7.4; Maven's fixed $20 a payout is subtracted from each payout instead, and Hola Prime's $5 minimum fee binds only on a payout under $200 of share, never at the chain's targets), and the refund \(R\) comes with the \(r\)-th payout (\(r=1\) at most firms; Blue Guardian and Hola Prime 4, Maven 3). No fee is returned at FundingPips Flex, FXIFY Classic, BrightFunded without its paid add-on, Alpha Capital ("All our evaluation plans are non-refundable"), Fintokei (its English pages state no return) and the futures firms, and none is assumed at GFT, whose pages read call the fee refundable without saying when. The amount withdrawn at a success is the balance then, or the firm's cap if that is smaller: \(m_i = \mathbb{E}[\min(\text{balance at success}, \text{cap}_i)]\). The chain uses \(\min(\mathbb{E}[\text{balance at success}], \text{cap}_i)\), which equals it when the cycle's target is at least the cap and is otherwise an upper bound (Jensen's inequality); version 9 used the uncapped balance even on GFT's capped cycles, so its GFT table paid more than the cap (review of version 9, finding 10). Version 8 wrote \(X_1\) and \(X\) here, the targets, while its chain used the balances at success; at FTMO's chosen setting the difference was $82 per paid cycle (review of version 8, finding 16). The mean cash of an account that is paid exactly \(n\) cycles is \(\alpha\,(m_1 + (n-1)\,m)\) plus the refund if \(n \ge r\) (less $20\(n\) at Maven); its actual cash is random around that. With two special first cycles (GFT: the first two payouts are capped, so the first two cycles have target \(X_a\), chance \(q_a\) and mean paid \(m_a\), later ones \(X_b\), \(q_b\), \(m_b\)):

Two special first cycles (GFT) $$\mathbb{E}[\text{paid cycles}] = q_a + q_a^2 + \frac{q_a^2\,q_b}{1-q_b} = q_a + \frac{q_a^2}{1-q_b}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[W] = m_a\,(q_a + q_a^2) + m_b\,\frac{q_a^2\,q_b}{1-q_b}.$$

(Here \(m_a\) is the capped withdrawal of the first two cycles; a balance above the cap stays in the account, and the chain, which restarts each cycle from zero, leaves that carry-over out; the engine carries it.) In the zero-cost idealised model with exact landing, \(m = X\), \(q = D/(X+D)\), \(\mathbb{E}[\text{paid cycles}] = D/X\) and \(\mathbb{E}[W] = D\), which is where the zero-cost example of 1.3 takes \(q\) from.

7.3 What the cycle target changes

In the idealised zero-cost model, nothing about the average. With costs, at fixed \(l\) and \(k\), the expected number of funded trades grows with \(X\): by 5.3 it is about \(XD/v\) per cycle times \((X+D)/X\) expected cycles, i.e. \(D(X+D)/v\). So a larger cycle means more trades and more cost over the account's life, and more time between payouts; a smaller one means more payout requests and more waiting for payout dates. Version 6 said larger cycles mean fewer trades and less cost; that contradicted this formula and is withdrawn. With caps, daily targets and payout calendars the balance between the two effects is found by search (Chapter 9), and the measured effect of \(X\) is shown in each programme's chapter.

7.4 Funded rules and what they change

  • Best-day rules. If no day may exceed a share \(\beta\) of a payout's profit, each day's take-profit is capped at \(\beta X\) less what the day has made (FXIFY 25%, measured against the account's best day ever; the plan's own 40% everywhere), and a payout is requested only when the rule holds.
  • Maven: "If your total profit exceeds $5,000, your best trading day ... or singular trade ... may not exceed 50% of your profit in the funded stage payout cycle", a "day" running until 24 hours pass between a close and the next entry. Under daily trading a week is one such day, so the plan keeps every Maven account's profit at or below $4,900, $100 under the threshold so that a take-profit filled a few points better than its price cannot bring the rule in, and the rule never applies. "Total profit" is read as the profit since the account was last reset, which Maven does after every payout; if it meant the profit of all cycles together, the rule would apply from the second cycle and would cut a cycle's profit whenever one "day" held more than half of it, which the model does not apply (the less strict of the two readings, marked in Appendix [[APP_INV]]). Maven also voids profit above $10,000 per 30-day cycle per trader, all accounts together, counted on the date the profit is closed; the plan holds one Maven account and keeps the profit closed in every 30-day window at or below $10,000, so no win is placed that could take a window above it (a keep-alive position's gain of a few cents above it is voided, as Maven states: 6.3), which meets the rule however the firm anchors its cycles.
  • Profitable days per cycle (FundingPips 95%: 3; Hola Prime: 3 within 14 days; GFT: 4; Maven: 3; Blue Guardian, stricter reading: 3) and trading days (Fintokei: 3 per payout; Alpha: 5 before the first): the daily target of 6.4, or ordinary trades one a day (6.3; no fillers at either).
  • Payout calendars. The first payout is allowed 14 to 30 days after the first trade on the funded account (FTMO, FundingPips, Fintokei, GFT, Alpha and Maven count from the first trade; the engine does so for every firm), then every 14 to 30 days (Maven's 10 business days are taken as 14 calendar days). Counting from the first trade is not "at most hours" later than counting from funding, as version 8 said: an account issued after Friday's entry cut-off has its first trade on Monday (review of version 8, finding 21). A cycle that reaches its target early waits, flat. Request, processing and receipt are separate times: the cash arrives after the firm's processing time in working days (6.6), and at Fintokei and The5ers the next request is allowed 14 days after the last payout was processed (Fintokei) or approved (The5ers), not after the request (review of version 8, finding 10). Maven allows a request every 10 business days; the engine uses 14 calendar days, the same span unless a public holiday falls inside it (holidays are not modelled).
  • Duration rules. Blue Guardian: "The minimum holding time is 2 minutes"; a shorter trade "may be flagged for tick scalping". The plan opens with only an emergency stop at the breach level and places its bracket 3 minutes after the entry, counted in minutes (version 9 counted sub-steps: review of version 9, finding 7); at that moment it closes at the market if the price is already beyond the stop or the target. Only a breach or the Guardian Shield can then end a trade sooner, and the engine treats a trade under 2 minutes as ending the account. Alpha Capital: an account's average trade must last over 2 minutes, most trades must, and at least half of the gross profit must come from trades over 2 minutes; the engine checks this on the account's own trades at every phase pass and payout request and applies the firm's consequence on failure: a passed evaluation must 'restart the evaluation from Phase 1', which the engine counts as a failed attempt whose slot buys the next one (the cautious reading: Alpha does not say whether the restart is free), and a qualified account's profits are removed. BrightFunded: a trading day counts only if a trade on it was open for at least 60 seconds. Version 8 relied on a distance argument and a global count instead (review of version 8, findings 6 and 7).
  • Guardian Shield (Blue Guardian's funded accounts): every open trade is closed at a floating loss of 2% of the account; the first time, the split falls to 50% for good, the second time the account is breached. A stop at 1.75% plus cost stays inside 2%, but during the 3-minute hold the position has no stop, and a position closed at the market when the hold ends can be beyond its stop. The engine applies the Shield at every minute of the hold and after it (version 9 left it out: review of version 9, finding 6). A position whose price is beyond its take-profit when the hold ends is closed at the market there, so such a win can exceed the plan's 40% cap by the overshoot ([[BG_OVER]]).
  • Alpha Capital's lot limit: at most 40 open lots per $100K on a qualified account (80 at 200K); the plan's single position is cut to the limit where its risk would need more (version 9 did not apply it: review of version 9, finding 4; [[ALPHA_LOTS]]).
  • GFT's minimum holding time (funded accounts bought from 6 October 2026): the profit of a trade held under a minute is removed; the engine counts such a win as minus its cost.
  • Minimum payouts: FTMO and FundedNext $20, FundingPips 1% of the account, FXIFY and Hola Prime $50, Fintokei, GFT and Blue Guardian $100, Topstep $125, The5ers $150, Apex $500, each applied to the trader's share after the split and any fee (next item), the stricter reading where a firm does not say which (and stricter than a minimum stated as profit). Alpha Capital and FunderPro state theirs as profit ("$100 gross, $80 net" at Alpha): a request needs $100 of profit before the split and $80 of share after it. A request under its minimum is not made, and the account trades on. The5ers also needs $500 of profit for a request on 100K ($300 on 50K). Version 9 had no minimum at FTMO, FundedNext or Hola Prime, and applied Alpha's and FunderPro's $100 to the share (n31, n38, n41).
  • Payout fees: five firms take a share of every payout and Maven a fixed amount, which version 10 models on the whole withdrawal, the refund included, with each minimum checked after it: GFT 3% after the split (its trader receives 97% of 80%, \(\alpha = 0.776\) of the profit withdrawn), The5ers a 3.5% commission on every cash withdrawal, FundedNext a processing fee of up to 3.5% on every request (3.5% modelled), Blue Guardian 3%, Hola Prime 2.5% on every method with at least $5 by crypto ($25 by bank wire or Rise), and Maven $20 a payout by Rise, its only named method outside South Africa, Nigeria, Kenya and Ghana (the firms' payout pages, read 12 October 2026; version 9 had none of them). Where a firm offers a payout method without a charge the plan uses it; banks' and networks' own costs and currency conversion are not modelled (Appendix [[APP_INV]] lists each firm's statement).
  • GFT's best trade (accounts bought from 9 October 2026): a reward needs the account's most profitable trade to have made more than its worst trade lost. The engine checks it at every request, both since the last payout and over the account's life, and trades on if it fails ([[GFT_BW]]).
  • Payout caps: GFT's first two payouts at most the smaller of 6% of the account and $10,000; The5ers at most $4,000 per payout on 100K ($3,000 on 50K; other sizes unpublished, 4% of the size assumed), with the fee refund counted inside the cap (the stricter reading of an unpublished basis); Topstep at most half the balance and $2,000; Apex $1,500, $1,500, $2,000, $2,500, $2,500 and $3,000 for its six payouts. A payout is the smaller of the profit and the cap, and the rest stays in the account (version 9 left out The5ers' cap and gate: review of version 9, finding 16). The5ers' account scales up at +10% of profit with three profitable days; its page does not say when the scaling takes effect, whether it can be declined or how it meets the payout cycle, so the plan keeps the balance under +9.5% and the account keeps the rules it was bought with.
  • Daily profit caps (GFT: $3,000 a day at every size): the take-profit is capped at what is left of the day's cap, so nothing is deducted.

Without costs, rules that only cap or delay wins leave the average withdrawal unchanged (a payout cap does not lose the excess, which stays in the account); with costs, each extra trade costs \(c\). A rule that removes profit (a deduction, a voided win, a cancelled payout) lowers it by \(\mathbb{E}[Q]\). The engine includes all of them.

Chapter 8

Time, and value per month

8.1 How long one trade lasts, approximately

For a driftless arithmetic Brownian motion with variance \(\sigma^2\) per hour, \(Z_t^2 - \sigma^2 t\) is a martingale, and stopping at \(+d_t\) or \(-d_s\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] = d_s d_t/\sigma^2\). For the bracket, \(d_s = m\sigma\) and \(d_t = m\sigma(w+c)/l\):

Expected duration of a bracket (hours of open market), approximation $$\mathbb{E}[\tau] \approx m^2\,\frac{w + c}{l}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\tau \mid \text{win}] \approx \frac{m^2}{3}\Big(\big(\tfrac{w+c}{l}\big)^2 + 2\,\tfrac{w+c}{l}\Big), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\tau \mid \text{loss}] \approx \frac{m^2}{3}\Big(1 + 2\,\tfrac{w+c}{l}\Big).$$

The synthetic price is geometric: its log has drift \(-\sigma^2/2\) per hour, and the barriers sit at log distances \(-\ln(1-s)\) and \(\ln(1+u)\), not \(s\) and \(u\). For the plan's brackets (\(s\) and \(u\) a fraction of a percent to a few percent) the difference is of the order of \(s\) and \(u\) themselves, a fraction of a percent of the duration. The daily flat also cuts long trades at 20:00 UTC. So these formulas are used only to explain orders of magnitude; every duration reported in Part II is measured by the engine. The same asymmetry makes a long and a short bracket with the same distances last different times: on the geometric price both win with chance \(s/(s+u)\), but with \(s = \sigma\) and \(u = 3\sigma\) on the Nasdaq a long lasts 2.9891 hours on average and a short 3.0110 (optional stopping on the log price: \(\mathbb{E}[T] = -2[p\ln(1\pm u) + (1-p)\ln(1\mp s)]/\sigma^2\); review of version 9, finding 24). So a zero expected profit per trade does not make two direction rules worth the same per month; Chapter 11 measures the difference instead of assuming it is zero.

8.2 How long a phase lasts

In the idealised model the number of trades is \(AB/v\) (5.3) with \(v = l(w+c)\), and each lasts about \(m^2(w+c)/l\) hours, so the market time is about \((A/l)(B/l)\,m^2\) hours, independent of \(k\). For FTMO phase 1 at \(m = 0.75\) and \(l = \$1{,}500\): \(6.67^2 \times 0.56 \approx 25\) hours of open market. The calendar is set by the rules instead: minimum and profitable days, the concentration policy's three winning days, the daily flat, weekends, reviews and payout dates.

8.3 Value per month: what is measured, and what it is not

The plan runs slots: one account position at a firm, refilled with a new evaluation as soon as an attempt ends. A slot completes attempts with values \(V_i\) and durations \(T_i\). If those pairs were independent and identically distributed, the renewal–reward theorem would give the long-run rate directly:

Renewal–reward theorem $$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n V_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n T_i} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[V]}{\mathbb{E}[T]}, \qquad \text{EV per month} = 30.44\times\frac{\mathbb{E}[V]}{\mathbb{E}[T\ \text{in days}]}.$$

They are not: a new attempt starts on the weekday and at the hour where the last one ended, at the price level where it ended, and at a few firms it carries a credit (The5ers) or a rolling window (Maven). Resetting the balance does not reset the calendar or the price. A Markov renewal argument would give a long-run rate \(\mathbb{E}_\pi[V]/\mathbb{E}_\pi[T]\) under a stationary distribution \(\pi\) of the start states, if there were one. In this model there is none: the price is a driftless geometric random walk, which has no stationary distribution (it tends to zero almost surely), and the price level enters every attempt through whole lots and contracts (the smallest position's risk, one micro contract's risk) and Alpha Capital's lot limit. Version 9 called its continuing-slot figure the long-run rate on the strength of that argument; the label is withdrawn (review of version 9, finding 11). Whether a long-run rate exists for this price-dependent system is not established here.

Two estimators. The search of Chapter 9 ranks settings by the random-start attempt ratio \(30.44\sum V_i/\sum T_i\) over attempts that each start a new trader at a random point of a path, at a random hour, with nothing carried over and a coin flip for each trade's direction. A random point is a random day of the first six years of a twelve-year path, whose price starts at today's level and wanders from there, so the attempts meet a range of price levels; version 8 described it as the stationary rate with credits and windows carried, which it is not (review of version 8, finding 12). The second estimator runs the plan as written, in continuing slots (continuing_v10.py): for every final setting, a slot buys an evaluation, trades it to its end, buys the next at once, and so on, for nine years on one path and calendar, carrying everything a real slot carries (the path position and price level, the calendar, The5ers' credits and the smaller refund they leave, monthly and activation fees, payouts dated when their cash arrives and taken back if a breach cancels them, and the plan's 5-hour direction rule). The first year is discarded. The figure is named for what it is: the continuing-slot rate, the average cash per month dated in years 2 to 9 of slots started in the first two years of paths that begin at today's prices (each synthetic path starts at the market's close of 2 October 2026). Twelve slots start on different days of each of 16 new paths (1151–1166). Because the price level drifts over nine years and matters, every chosen setting is also reported with years 2–5 against years 6–9 (paired by path) and with the starting price at half and at twice today's level. Part III uses the continuing-slot rate to choose between a firm's programmes, markets and sizes, because it runs the plan as written; it is not a proven indefinite rate. The random-start ratio stays the search's yardstick, and Chapter 11 compares the two with matched estimands (cont_check_v10.py).

The random-start ratio is a ratio of totals (a ratio of expectations, not the expectation of ratios), and the objective each programme's settings are chosen for; the continuing-slot rate is the mean over paths of each path's cash per month. Each is reported with the interval of its own estimator (8.4), and a difference is taken only between like estimators (review of version 9, finding 23).

8.4 How precise a simulated figure is

With \(n\) independent attempts the ratio estimate \(\hat r = \bar V/\bar T\) has, by the delta method, standard error \(\operatorname{sd}(V_i - \hat r\,T_i)/(\sqrt{n}\,\bar T)\). Attempts that start at random times on the same synthetic path share its history, so they are not fully independent. The final figures therefore use 16 independent paths per programme (1,500 attempts on each) and treat each path as a cluster:

Cluster-robust interval for a ratio $$\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat r) = \frac{G}{G-1}\,\frac{\sum_{g=1}^{G}\big(V_g - \hat r\,T_g\big)^2}{\big(\sum_g T_g\big)^2},$$

where \(V_g, T_g\) are the totals on path \(g\). With only \(G\) clusters the multiplier for a 95% interval is the Student \(t\) quantile with \(G-1\) degrees of freedom, not 1.96: 2.131 for 16 paths, 2.069 for 24, 2.012 for 48, 3.182 for 4, computed (version 8 took 2.060 for 24 paths from a table's next row). Every "±" for a programme is the larger of that cluster interval and 1.96 attempt-level standard errors; a pass rate gets the same treatment, with \(V_g\) the passes and \(T_g\) the attempts on path \(g\) (version 8 printed a binomial interval over all attempts: review of version 8, finding 14). A continuing slot's rate is the mean of its 16 per-path rates, with the same Student interval. Every validation check of Chapter 11 follows the same rule: it runs on several independent paths (four at least, mostly 16 or 48) and takes its interval over the paths, never over trades that revisit one path's history (version 9 took the intervals of its bracket checks over 60,000 trades on four paths and of its scaling test over the attempts of one path; reproduced, the bracket intervals were 2.3 to 2.9 times too narrow: review of version 9, finding 22).

Sums and differences. Every programme's final and continuing figures come from the same 16 paths, so programmes on one market, and sizes of one programme, are correlated. The interval of a sum of programmes (a portfolio's sum of continuing-slot rates) is therefore computed from the per-path totals of all of them together, which carries their full covariance, \(\operatorname{Var}(\sum_j R_j) = \sum_j \operatorname{Var}(R_j) + 2\sum_{j<k}\operatorname{Cov}(R_j, R_k)\), rather than by adding the programmes' widths in quadrature as version 8 did (review of version 8, finding 15). Likewise two settings run on the same paths and random numbers are compared by their paired per-path difference, of the same estimator on both sides (a ratio of totals against a ratio of totals, each path's contribution taken by the delta method; a mean of per-path rates against the same): its interval can be narrow when their separate intervals overlap. The chain checks of Chapter 11 use 48 independent paths per programme for the same reason: with thousands of attempts on one path, the same stretch of price history is traded dozens of times, and an attempt-level interval would be several times too narrow. Portfolio lives each run on their own new path, so they are independent and the ordinary standard error applies. These are Monte Carlo intervals under the model: they say how well the model's expectation is estimated, not how much a real year can differ from it, and they carry no allowance for wrong rules, wrong costs or a wrong model.

8.5 A first year is not the long-run rate

A new portfolio pays every fee in its first weeks and receives its first payouts a month or more later; at the end of any finite horizon some accounts are part-way through and hold value not yet paid. So the first year's average cash, the average of later months, and the continuing-slot rate are three different quantities, and nothing requires them to be equal. Chapter [[CH_LOCK]] reports them separately: the first-year average from 12-month lives, and the average of months 13–36 and the value of the accounts still open at month 12 from 36-month lives. The months 13–36 average is a finite-window figure: Chapter [[CH_LOCK]].6 reports its two annual windows with a paired interval; it is the average of those months under the model, not a proven long-run rate.
