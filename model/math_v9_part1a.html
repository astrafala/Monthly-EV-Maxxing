Part I

The mathematics, from zero

Every result in this document follows from a handful of facts about fair games. This part derives each of them, says exactly which model each formula is exact for, and works the numbers of the plan through at every step. Where a formula is an approximation it is labelled as one, and the engine (the rule-by-rule simulation) supplies the figure that is used.

Chapter 1

What is being bought

A proprietary trading firm ("prop firm") sells an evaluation. You pay a fee, you trade a simulated account under written rules, and if you meet the profit targets without breaking a loss limit, the firm gives you a funded account. On the funded account you keep a share of every profit you withdraw, and the firm absorbs every loss. You never owe the firm money beyond the fee.

That last sentence is the whole opportunity. Your downside is capped at the fee, and your upside is a share of profits that, as Chapter 7 shows, have a positive expected size even when every trade is a coin flip. The contract is an option: you pay a premium (the fee) for the right to keep part of the gains of a game whose losses fall on someone else.

1.2 The value of one attempt

Call the cash an attempt brings you \(V\). It is the fee (always paid), plus, if both phases are passed, whatever the funded account pays out (the refund included):

Value of one attempt $$V = -F + \mathbf{1}\{\text{pass}\}\cdot C, \qquad \mathbb{E}[V] = -F + \Pr(\text{pass})\cdot \mathbb{E}[C \mid \text{pass}]$$

Here \(\mathbf{1}\{\text{pass}\}\) is 1 if both phases are passed and 0 otherwise, and \(C\) is the total cash from the funded account. Where a firm returns part of the fee as non-cash credit when a phase is passed (The5ers), that credit is a third term, counted separately from cash (Chapter [[CH0_T5]]).

1.3 The whole argument in five lines, in an idealised model

Before the details, the result in its simplest form: no trading costs, every phase ending exactly at its target or its floor, and payouts of a fixed cycle target \(X\) from a static allowance. Chapter 5 proves that a fair game reaches \(+A\) before \(-B\) with probability \(B/(A+B)\) in that model; Chapter 7 proves that a funded account then withdraws on average exactly \(D\) before it is lost, and reaches its first payout with probability \(q = D/(D+X)\). The refund comes only with that first payout, so it is weighted by \(q\):

FTMO 2-Step, zero cost, zero skill, idealised $$\Pr(\text{pass}) = \frac{B}{A_1+B}\cdot\frac{B}{A_2+B} = \frac{10{,}000}{20{,}000}\cdot\frac{10{,}000}{15{,}000} = \frac{1}{3}$$ $$\mathbb{E}[V] = -F + \Pr(\text{pass})\big(\alpha D + q\,R\big) = -632 + \tfrac13\Big(8{,}000 + \tfrac{10{,}000}{10{,}000 + X}\,632\Big)$$

At the version 6 fee of $632 this is $2,140.00 with a cycle of \(X = \$10{,}000\) and $2,087.33 with \(X = \$30{,}000\); version 6 printed $2,245, which counts the refund on every pass and is only the limit as \(X \to 0\). At the correct fee of $607.82 and \(X = \$10{,}000\) it is \(-607.82 + \tfrac13(8{,}000 + 303.91) = \$2{,}160.15\).

Everything else in this document refines this number: trading costs lower it; the plan's policies (flat every evening, at most 40% of a target from one trade or one day, at least 10 minutes between trades, targets and stops at least 0.6 hourly standard deviations from the entry, whole lots, and a last trade with its stop at the floor when an account has too little room left) change both the value and the time; and the settings are then chosen to make the most of it per month and per person. The sign is already visible here; its size needs the full engine.

1.4 Why the firm still makes money, and what that implies

The firm's revenue is the fees of everyone, and most traders are not zero-skill traders with a plan built around the contract: they over-trade, break rules, abandon accounts and stop after one failure. A plan that treats the contract as a mathematical object and repeats it many times is the case the firm's pricing does not cover. Firms know this, and their terms contain behavioural rules (against "gambling", "all-in" trading, one-trade target attainment, account rolling) that are written broadly and applied at their discretion. The plan is built to meet every rule that can be stated as a condition on trades, and Chapter [[CH_RISK]] lists the behavioural rules that cannot, firm by firm, as conditions under which the figures here apply.

Chapter 2

The probability toolkit

Seven ideas carry the analysis. Each is stated with the conditions it needs.

2.1 Expected value and linearity

A random quantity \(X\) that takes values \(x_1, x_2, \dots\) with probabilities \(p_1, p_2, \dots\) has expected value \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_i p_i\, x_i\). For any random quantities, dependent or not,

$$\mathbb{E}[X+Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y], \qquad \mathbb{E}[cX] = c\,\mathbb{E}[X].$$

Linearity says that the expected cash of many accounts over one horizon, under one policy is the sum of their expected cash over that horizon, however strongly they move together. It does not say that a first year's average equals a long-run rate (they are different quantities, Chapter 8.5), and it does not make correlation irrelevant: correlation leaves the sum of the means alone and changes everything else (the spread, the cash needed for fees, the chance of many failures at once).

2.2 Variance

\(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2\). For independent \(X, Y\) variances add; for dependent ones a term \(2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\) is added. Variance enters the time a fair game takes (Chapter 8) and the confidence intervals of every simulated figure.

2.3 Fair games and martingales

A sequence of balances \(X_0, X_1, \dots\) is a martingale if \(\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid X_0,\dots,X_n] = X_n\). "Zero skill" means this for the balance before costs: the price is a martingale, the balance is a sum of positions times price changes, and no rule for direction, size or timing that uses only the past can change that. With costs the expected next balance is lower by the cost: a supermartingale.

2.4 Stopping times and the optional stopping theorem

A stopping time \(\tau\) is a rule for when to stop that uses only information available at the time. The optional stopping theorem: if \(X\) is a martingale, \(\tau\) is almost surely finite, and the stopped process \(X_{n\wedge\tau}\) is bounded (or, more generally, uniformly integrable), then

$$\mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = X_0 .$$

Each use below states why the condition holds. For one phase it holds because the balance stays between the floor and the target plus one win. For a funded account whose profits are withdrawn the balance plus everything withdrawn is not bounded, so Chapter 7 applies the theorem to one payout cycle at a time, where it is.

2.5 Wald's identity

If the steps \(Y_1, Y_2, \dots\) are independent and identically distributed with finite mean \(\mu\), and \(\tau\) is a stopping time with \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] < \infty\), then \(\mathbb{E}[\sum_{n\le\tau} Y_n] = \mu\,\mathbb{E}[\tau]\). Having the same unconditional mean at every step is not enough: with \(Y_1 = \pm1\) equally likely, \(Y_2 = -Y_1\), and stopping after the first step if \(Y_1 = +1\) and after the second otherwise, every step has mean 0 but the stopped sum has mean \(\tfrac12\). The form used here is the martingale one: if the conditional mean of each step given the past is \(-c_n\) (a cost known when the trade is placed), the conditional absolute means are bounded and \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] < \infty\), then \(\mathbb{E}[X_\tau - X_0] = -\mathbb{E}[\sum_{n\le\tau} c_n]\), because \(X_n + \sum_{i\le n} c_i\) is a martingale with bounded increments in mean and optional stopping applies. Every trade of the plan has conditional mean minus its own cost given everything before it (4.3), so this holds even when the cost varies from trade to trade.

2.6 Brownian motion

The continuous-time limit of a fair random walk is Brownian motion \(W_t\): it starts at 0, has independent increments, and \(W_{t+h} - W_t\) is normal with mean 0 and variance \(h\). A process \(X_t = \mu t + \sigma W_t\) has three martingales that carry the approximations of Chapters 5 and 8:

$$X_t - \mu t, \qquad (X_t-\mu t)^2 - \sigma^2 t, \qquad \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{2\mu}{\sigma^2} X_t\right).$$

The third follows from Itô's formula: \(d e^{\lambda X} = e^{\lambda X}(\lambda\mu + \tfrac12\lambda^2\sigma^2)\,dt + (\dots)\,dW\), whose \(dt\) term vanishes for \(\lambda = -2\mu/\sigma^2\). These formulas are exact for Brownian motion; applied to a sum of bracket trades, which jump, they are approximations (5.2).

2.7 The reflection principle

For a Brownian path from \(a\) to \(b\) over a time with variance \(v\), the chance that it rose above \(y \ge \max(a,b)\) in between is

$$\Pr\big(\max \ge y \,\big|\, a \to b\big) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{2(y-a)(y-b)}{v}\right).$$

This gives the marginal distribution of the maximum of a bridge (and, by symmetry, of the minimum). The maximum and the minimum of one bridge are dependent, and the marginal alone does not say which of a high and a low level was touched first. Section 3.3 bounds the error of drawing them independently. For two levels \(\ell < a, b < u\) the exact chance of staying between them is the image series \(\sum_{k}\big[e^{-2kW(kW - (b-a))/v} - e^{-2(a-\ell-kW)(b-\ell-kW)/v}\big]\), \(W = u - \ell\), which tests_v9.py uses to check the bound.

Chapter 3

The price model

3.1 Hourly volatility

Over one hour the log price changes by a random amount with standard deviation \(\sigma\), the hourly volatility, measured on two years of real hourly bars (Yahoo Finance, 2024–26, only hours that follow another trading hour):

[[SIGMA_TABLE]]

Over \(h\) hours the standard deviation grows like \(\sigma\sqrt h\): the Nasdaq moves about [[SIG_DAY]] in a 23-hour trading day. Every stop in the plan is a multiple of \(\sigma\), so the same rules fit every market.

3.2 Zero drift, stated precisely

The model gives the price no expected change: \(\mathbb{E}[P_{t+h} \mid P_t] = P_t\). That is the zero-skill assumption, and it is a modelling choice, not a theorem. For the price itself to be a martingale, the log price must drift down by \(\sigma^2/2\) per hour (about 0.0004% for the Nasdaq): \(\log P_{t+h} - \log P_t\) is normal with mean \(-\sigma^2 h/2\) and variance \(\sigma^2 h\). The Nasdaq's real long-run rise (about 0.0025% per hour, a hundredth of \(\sigma\)) is left out. Version 7 justified that by saying the plan is long and short equally often; equal counts do not settle it, because the drift's effect depends on the signed exposure over each trade's holding time. In the model the direction of every account's first position is chosen by a rule that is symmetric in up and down moves, so, with the price a martingale, a real drift would add its effect to longs and subtract it from shorts in expectation; the plan's results are therefore stated for the zero-drift model, and a real drift is one of the things that could make them wrong (Chapter [[CH_RISK]].6).

3.3 Synthetic paths

  1. Each hour is split into 60 one-minute sub-steps of normal increments of the log price with variance \(\sigma^2/60\) and mean \(-\sigma^2/120\) (version 7: 12 five-minute sub-steps).
  2. Within each sub-step, the highest and the lowest point are drawn from the exact marginal distributions of a Brownian bridge between the sub-step's end points (2.7): $$\max = \tfrac12\left(a+b+\sqrt{(b-a)^2 - 2v\ln U}\right),\qquad U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1),$$ and the minimum is the mirror image with an independent draw. Each is exact on its own, so a single level (a stop alone, or the account's breach level alone during a holding time) is crossed with exactly the right probability; drawing the two independently is not exact jointly, because a bridge that rises far is a little less likely to fall far as well. The sub-step's closing price is kept too: a position closed at a fixed time (a filler trade, the end of a holding time) is closed at it.
  3. When a stop and a target both fall inside one hourly bar, the engine replays that hour's sixty sub-steps in time order and takes the level the path touched first; it also uses the exit minute for the 10-minute gap between trades (10.4) and for the trade's duration. Only if both levels fall inside the same one-minute sub-step does it decide by the martingale chance from the sub-step's starting price, which is not the exact conditional probability either; its error lies inside the bound of the next item.
  4. How large the error can be. Couple the generator with an exact Brownian path that has the same sub-step end points (their law is exact). Given end points \(a, b\) inside a bracket \([\ell, u]\) of log-width \(W\), a sub-step's outcome (no touch, stop, target) can differ between the two only on the event that both barriers are touched, so the total-variation error is at most \(P_\text{ind} + P_\text{exact}\), where \(P_\text{ind} = e^{-2[(a-\ell)(b-\ell) + (u-a)(u-b)]/v}\) is the generator's chance and \(P_\text{exact}\) the bridge's. By the strong Markov property at the first touch of \(\ell\), the bridge then runs from \(\ell\) to \(b\) and reaches \(u\) with chance at most \(e^{-2W(u-b)/v}\), so \(P_\text{exact} \le e^{-2[(a-\ell)(b-\ell) + W(u-b)]/v} + e^{-2[(u-a)(u-b) + W(b-\ell)]/v} \le 2P_\text{ind}\): the error is at most \(3P_\text{ind}\) (with an end point outside the bracket, at most twice the chance of touching the far barrier, which is smaller). Version 8 claimed \(P_\text{ind} \le e^{-W^2/v}\), arguing that the exponent is smallest with both end points at the middle; that is true only for a sub-step with no net move, and false when the end points lie near opposite barriers: with \(\ell = 0\), \(u = 1\), \(a = 0.01\), \(b = 0.99\), \(v = 0.01\), \(P_\text{ind} = e^{-3.96} = 0.019\) and the exact chance is \(e^{-4} = 0.0183\), against a claimed \(e^{-100}\) (review of version 8, finding 2). For a net move \(\delta = b - a\) the largest \(P_\text{ind}\) over the starting point is \(e^{-(W^2-\delta^2)/v}\) (at \(a = \ell + (W-\delta)/2\)). The move is independent of where the sub-step starts and of whether the trade is still open, so each sub-step in which a trade watches both barriers adds at most $$G(r) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\min\big(1,\ 3e^{-(r^2 - Z^2)}\big)\,\mathbf 1\{|Z| \le r\} + \mathbf 1\{|Z| > r\}\Big] = \frac{6}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-(r^2+\ln 3)/2}\,J(x) + \operatorname{erfc}\!\big(x/\sqrt2\big),$$ with \(r = W/\sqrt v\) (less the tiny drift per sub-step), \(Z\) standard normal, \(x = \sqrt{r^2 - \ln 3}\) and \(J(x) = \int_0^x e^{(t^2 - x^2)/2}dt\); \(G(r)\) is close to \(e^{-r^2/2}\), the square root of version 8's figure. By the union bound over sub-steps, a trade's outcome differs from an exact path's with probability at most \(G(r)\) times its expected number of such sub-steps. At the plan's narrowest bracket (stop and target 0.6 hourly standard deviations each, \(r \approx 1.2\sqrt{60} = 9.3\)), \(G \approx 5\times10^{-20}\) per minute. The engine adds this bound up over every simulated trade (Chapter 11). It is a bound on the generator's error under the model, not a claim that the construction is exact.
  5. Markets that close at weekends are closed in the path from Friday to Sunday, but the price keeps moving underneath, so Monday can open with a gap. Under the plan's daily flat (6.5) no position is open then.
  6. Random streams. Every path is drawn from its own stream of random numbers, a numpy SeedSequence keyed by the path number, the market group, the length, the sub-steps per hour and the correlation. Each stage has its own path numbers: the grid search 101–104, the refinement 111–114, the final value of every chosen setting 121–136 (sixteen paths), the continuing slots 151–166, the sensitivities 141–144, the checks 401–6131, the rule tests 901–905, and the portfolio simulation one new path number per simulated life (100,000 and up; 300,000 and up for three-year lives). Gold, USDJPY and EURUSD use the life's number with their own key, so their paths are independent of the Nasdaq and of each other. Version 7 gave other markets offset numbers (+50, +60, +70), which made, for example, gold's validation paths reuse the random numbers of yen's search paths; streams_v9.py lists every stream of every stage and checks that no two independent stages share one (stream_manifest_v9.json). The Nasdaq CFD and the micro Nasdaq future share one stream on purpose: they follow the same index. Chapter [[CH_LOCK]] measures what a correlation of 0.3 between all four markets changes.

3.4 What the model leaves out

Real prices have volatility that changes (calm and wild months), fat tails, jumps at news, and spreads that widen at the daily break and around releases. The plan's stops and targets are at least 0.6 hourly standard deviations from the entry, so that average costs are a fair description, and the cost stress tests of Chapter [[CH_RISK]] double every cost. The synthetic model is the right tool for the question asked (what is the contract worth to a trader with no edge), not a forecast of any particular year.
