Chapter 7

What a funded account pays

On the funded account the plan trades each payout cycle to a profit target \(X\) above the starting balance, stops, requests the payout on the first allowed day, and withdraws everything above the start. The balance is back at the start, the static floor is still \(D\) below it, and the next cycle begins. If a payout date comes before the target, the plan withdraws whatever profit there is (where the firm's rules allow) and starts a new cycle; the cycle formulas below describe the simpler policy of paying only at the target, and the engine simulates the plan's. The account ends in a breach at the floor under the rule of 5.4 (or, at a few firms, after a fixed number of payouts).

7.1 The withdrawal identity, with its conditions

Take one cycle. Its balance (relative to the start) is a stopped sum of trades, bounded between \(-D\) and \(X\) plus one win, and each trade has conditional mean minus its own cost, so optional stopping applies to it (2.5). Let cycle \(i\) end with balance \(Z_i\) after costs \(K_i\), and let \(Q_i \ge 0\) be any profit removed from the account without being paid to the trader or counted as a cost (a firm's deduction of profit above a day cap, profit voided by a rolling cap). Then \(\mathbb{E}[Z_i] = -\mathbb{E}[K_i] - \mathbb{E}[Q_i]\). The account's total withdrawal is \(W = \sum_i Z_i\,\mathbf{1}\{\text{cycle } i \text{ paid}\}\) and it ends with balance \(X_{\text{end}}\) in its last cycle. Adding the cycles (the number of cycles has finite mean because each fails with a probability bounded away from zero):

The withdrawal identity, general form $$\mathbb{E}[W] = -\,\mathbb{E}[X_{\text{end}}] - \mathbb{E}[\text{total funded cost}] - \mathbb{E}[Q].$$

External credits (The5ers' hub credit, refunds) are paid outside the account and are added separately. Under the plan \(Q = 0\) in the model: wins are capped at what is left of GFT's daily cap and of Maven's ceiling and 30-day window, and no position is open through a gap, so nothing is deducted or voided. (In practice a take-profit filled a few points better than its price at an exact cap gives up those few points.) Under the terminal rule of 5.4 every account ends at the floor, \(X_\text{end} = -D\), and the identity becomes \(\mathbb{E}[W] = D - \mathbb{E}[\text{cost}]\), the form version 6 stated without its conditions. It fails when the account can end elsewhere: a daily-limit breach part-way down, a closure after a fixed number of payouts (Apex), or a floor that moves (Topstep, Apex); for those accounts only the engine's figures are used. The cycle-by-cycle argument is needed because the balance plus withdrawals is not bounded.

7.2 Cycle by cycle

Each cycle is a fresh run of the chain of 5.5 from 0 to its target \(X_i\) before the floor, with success chance \(q_i\). Cycles continue until one fails, so cycle \(j\) is paid with probability \(\prod_{i\le j} q_i\). A win may pass the target (the minimum target distance of 4.1), so a successful cycle pays its balance at success, not exactly \(X_i\): write \(m_i = \mathbb{E}[\text{balance at success of cycle } i \mid \text{it succeeds}]\), which the chain gives as \(\mathbb{E}[X_\text{end}\mid\text{pass}]\) of 5.5. With a first cycle \((X_1, q_1, m_1)\) and later ones \((X, q, m)\), in the chain's model of independent cycles that each restart from zero:

Funded cash, cycle form $$\mathbb{E}[C] = \alpha\left(m_1 q_1 + m\,\frac{q_1\,q}{1-q}\right) + R\cdot q_1 q^{\,r-1},$$

where the refund \(R\) comes with the \(r\)-th payout (\(r=1\) at most firms; Blue Guardian 4, Maven 3; FundingPips Flex, FXIFY Classic and BrightFunded without its paid add-on return no fee, and none is assumed at Alpha Pro); a refund in \(r\) equal parts with the first \(r\) payouts (Hola Prime) contributes \(\frac{R}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r} q_1 q^{\,j-1}\). Version 8 wrote \(X_1\) and \(X\) here, the targets, while its chain used the balances at success; at FTMO's chosen setting the difference was $82 per paid cycle (review of version 8, finding 16). The mean cash of an account that is paid exactly \(n\) cycles is \(\alpha\,(m_1 + (n-1)\,m)\) plus the refund if \(n \ge r\); its actual cash is random around that. With two special first cycles (GFT: the first two payouts are capped, so the first two cycles have target \(X_a\), chance \(q_a\) and mean paid \(m_a\), later ones \(X_b\), \(q_b\), \(m_b\)):

Two special first cycles (GFT) $$\mathbb{E}[\text{paid cycles}] = q_a + q_a^2 + \frac{q_a^2\,q_b}{1-q_b} = q_a + \frac{q_a^2}{1-q_b}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[W] = m_a\,(q_a + q_a^2) + m_b\,\frac{q_a^2\,q_b}{1-q_b}.$$

(GFT pays at most its cap in each of the first two payouts; a balance above the cap stays in the account, and the chain, which restarts each cycle from zero, leaves that carry-over out.) In the zero-cost idealised model with exact landing, \(m = X\), \(q = D/(X+D)\), \(\mathbb{E}[\text{paid cycles}] = D/X\) and \(\mathbb{E}[W] = D\), which is where the zero-cost example of 1.3 takes \(q\) from.

7.3 What the cycle target changes

In the idealised zero-cost model, nothing about the average. With costs, at fixed \(l\) and \(k\), the expected number of funded trades grows with \(X\): by 5.3 it is about \(XD/v\) per cycle times \((X+D)/X\) expected cycles, i.e. \(D(X+D)/v\). So a larger cycle means more trades and more cost over the account's life, and more time between payouts; a smaller one means more payout requests and more waiting for payout dates. Version 6 said larger cycles mean fewer trades and less cost; that contradicted this formula and is withdrawn. With caps, daily targets and payout calendars the balance between the two effects is found by search (Chapter 9), and the measured effect of \(X\) is shown in each programme's chapter.

7.4 Funded rules and what they change

  • Best-day rules. If no day may exceed a share \(\beta\) of a payout's profit, each day's take-profit is capped at \(\beta X\) less what the day has made (FXIFY 25%, measured against the account's best day ever; the plan's own 40% everywhere), and a payout is requested only when the rule holds.
  • Maven: above $5,000 of profit, no "day" (a run of trades without a 24-hour gap) and no trade may exceed 50% of the cycle's profit. Under daily trading a week is one such day, so the plan keeps every Maven account's profit at or below $4,900, $100 under the threshold so that a take-profit filled a few points better than its price cannot bring the rule in, and the rule never applies. At most $10,000 of profit may be closed in any 30 days, the excess voided, so no win is placed that could take any window above it.
  • Profitable days per cycle (FundingPips 95%: 3; Hola Prime: 3 within 14 days; GFT: 4; Maven: 3; Blue Guardian, stricter reading: 3) and trading days (Fintokei: 3 per payout; Alpha: 5 before the first): the daily target of 6.4, or ordinary trades one a day (6.3; no fillers at either).
  • Payout calendars. The first payout is allowed 14 to 30 days after the first trade on the funded account (FTMO, FundingPips, Fintokei, GFT, Alpha and Maven count from the first trade; the engine does so for every firm), then every 10 to 30 days. Counting from the first trade is not "at most hours" later than counting from funding, as version 8 said: an account issued after Friday's entry cut-off has its first trade on Monday (review of version 8, finding 21). A cycle that reaches its target early waits, flat. Request, processing and receipt are separate times: the cash arrives after the firm's processing time in working days (6.6), and at Fintokei the next request is allowed 14 days after the last payout was successfully processed, not after the request (review of version 8, finding 10).
  • Duration rules. Blue Guardian: "The minimum holding time is 2 minutes"; a shorter trade "may be flagged for tick scalping". The plan opens with only an emergency stop at the breach level and places its bracket 3 minutes after the entry; at that moment it closes at the market if the price is already beyond the stop or the target. Only a breach can then end a trade sooner, and the engine treats a trade under 2 minutes as ending the account. Alpha Capital: an account's average trade must last over 2 minutes, most trades must, and at least half of the gross profit must come from trades over 2 minutes; the engine checks this on the account's own trades at every phase pass and payout request and applies the firm's consequence on failure (the evaluation restarts; a qualified account's profits are removed). BrightFunded: a trading day counts only if a trade on it was open for at least 60 seconds. Version 8 relied on a distance argument and a global count instead (review of version 8, findings 6 and 7).
  • Minimum payouts: FundingPips 1% of the account, FXIFY $50, Fintokei, GFT, FunderPro, Blue Guardian and Alpha $100, The5ers $150.
  • Payout caps (GFT: the first two payouts at most 6% or $10,000): the first two cycles aim at the cap.
  • Daily profit caps (GFT: $3,000 a day at every size): the take-profit is capped at what is left of the day's cap, so nothing is deducted.

Without costs, rules that only cap or delay wins leave the average withdrawal unchanged; with costs, each extra trade costs \(c\). The engine includes all of them.

Chapter 8

Time, and value per month

8.1 How long one trade lasts, approximately

For a driftless arithmetic Brownian motion with variance \(\sigma^2\) per hour, \(Z_t^2 - \sigma^2 t\) is a martingale, and stopping at \(+d_t\) or \(-d_s\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] = d_s d_t/\sigma^2\). For the bracket, \(d_s = m\sigma\) and \(d_t = m\sigma(w+c)/l\):

Expected duration of a bracket (hours of open market), approximation $$\mathbb{E}[\tau] \approx m^2\,\frac{w + c}{l}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\tau \mid \text{win}] \approx \frac{m^2}{3}\Big(\big(\tfrac{w+c}{l}\big)^2 + 2\,\tfrac{w+c}{l}\Big), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\tau \mid \text{loss}] \approx \frac{m^2}{3}\Big(1 + 2\,\tfrac{w+c}{l}\Big).$$

The synthetic price is geometric: its log has drift \(-\sigma^2/2\) per hour, and the barriers sit at log distances \(-\ln(1-s)\) and \(\ln(1+u)\), not \(s\) and \(u\). For the plan's brackets (\(s\) and \(u\) a fraction of a percent to a few percent) the difference is of the order of \(s\) and \(u\) themselves, a fraction of a percent of the duration. The daily flat also cuts long trades at 20:00 UTC. So these formulas are used only to explain orders of magnitude; every duration reported in Part II is measured by the engine.

8.2 How long a phase lasts

In the idealised model the number of trades is \(AB/v\) (5.3) with \(v = l(w+c)\), and each lasts about \(m^2(w+c)/l\) hours, so the market time is about \((A/l)(B/l)\,m^2\) hours, independent of \(k\). For FTMO phase 1 at \(m = 0.75\) and \(l = \$1{,}500\): \(6.67^2 \times 0.56 \approx 25\) hours of open market. The calendar is set by the rules instead: minimum and profitable days, the concentration policy's three winning days, the daily flat, weekends, reviews and payout dates.

8.3 Value per month: the renewal–reward theorem, and how it is estimated

The plan runs slots: one account position at a firm, refilled with a new evaluation as soon as an attempt ends. A slot completes attempts with values \(V_i\) and durations \(T_i\). If those pairs were independent and identically distributed, the renewal–reward theorem would give the long-run rate directly:

Renewal–reward theorem $$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n V_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n T_i} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[V]}{\mathbb{E}[T]}, \qquad \text{EV per month} = 30.44\times\frac{\mathbb{E}[V]}{\mathbb{E}[T\ \text{in days}]}.$$

They are not quite: a new attempt starts on the weekday and at the hour where the last one ended, on futures at the price level where it ended, and at a few firms it carries a credit (The5ers) or a rolling window (Maven). Resetting the balance does not reset the calendar. What holds instead is a Markov renewal argument: with \(S_i\) the state at the start of attempt \(i\) (position in the week, price level, carried credit or window), the long-run rate is \(\mathbb{E}_\pi[V]/\mathbb{E}_\pi[T]\), the ratio of the means under the stationary distribution \(\pi\) of the start states, provided that chain is ergodic.

Two estimators. The search of Chapter 9 ranks settings by the random-start attempt ratio \(30.44\sum V_i/\sum T_i\) over attempts that each start a new trader at a random point of a path, at a random hour, with nothing carried over and a random direction rule. Random starts are not distributed as \(\pi\), and nothing proves the two ratios equal; version 8 described the random-start ratio as the stationary rate with credits and windows carried, which it is not (review of version 8, finding 12). Version 9 therefore measures the long-run rate directly with continuing slots (continuing_v9.py): for every final setting, a slot buys an evaluation, trades it to its end, buys the next at once, and so on, for nine years on one path and calendar, carrying everything a real slot carries (the path position and price level, the calendar, The5ers' credits, monthly and activation fees, payouts dated when they arrive, and the plan's 5-hour direction rule); the first year is a warm-up and is discarded, and the rate is the cash dated in years 2 to 9. Twelve slots start on different days of each of 16 new paths (151–166). The continuing rate is Part II's long-run rate and what Part III's choices use; the random-start ratio stays the search's yardstick, and Chapter 11 compares the two for every chosen setting.

This is a long-run rate: a ratio of expectations, not the expectation of ratios, and the objective each programme's settings are chosen for.

8.4 How precise a simulated figure is

With \(n\) independent attempts the ratio estimate \(\hat r = \bar V/\bar T\) has, by the delta method, standard error \(\operatorname{sd}(V_i - \hat r\,T_i)/(\sqrt{n}\,\bar T)\). Attempts that start at random times on the same synthetic path share its history, so they are not fully independent. The final figures therefore use 16 independent paths per programme (1,500 attempts on each) and treat each path as a cluster:

Cluster-robust interval for a ratio $$\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat r) = \frac{G}{G-1}\,\frac{\sum_{g=1}^{G}\big(V_g - \hat r\,T_g\big)^2}{\big(\sum_g T_g\big)^2},$$

where \(V_g, T_g\) are the totals on path \(g\). With only \(G\) clusters the multiplier for a 95% interval is the Student \(t\) quantile with \(G-1\) degrees of freedom, not 1.96: 2.131 for 16 paths, 2.069 for 24, 3.182 for 4, computed (version 8 took 2.060 for 24 paths from a table's next row). Every "±" for a programme is the larger of that cluster interval and 1.96 attempt-level standard errors; a pass rate gets the same treatment, with \(V_g\) the passes and \(T_g\) the attempts on path \(g\) (version 8 printed a binomial interval over all attempts: review of version 8, finding 14). A continuing slot's rate is the mean of its 16 per-path rates, with the same Student interval.

Sums and differences. Every programme's final and continuing figures come from the same 16 paths, so programmes on one market, and sizes of one programme, are correlated. The interval of a sum of programmes (a portfolio's sum of long-run rates) is therefore computed from the per-path totals of all of them together, which carries their full covariance, \(\operatorname{Var}(\sum_j R_j) = \sum_j \operatorname{Var}(R_j) + 2\sum_{j<k}\operatorname{Cov}(R_j, R_k)\), rather than by adding the programmes' widths in quadrature as version 8 did (review of version 8, finding 15). Likewise two settings run on the same paths and random numbers are compared by their paired per-path difference: its interval can be narrow when their separate intervals overlap. The checks of Chapter 11 use 24 independent paths per programme for the same reason: with thousands of attempts on one path, the same stretch of price history is traded dozens of times, and an attempt-level interval would be several times too narrow. Portfolio lives each run on their own new path, so they are independent and the ordinary standard error applies. These are Monte Carlo intervals under the model: they say how well the model's expectation is estimated, not how much a real year can differ from it, and they carry no allowance for wrong rules, wrong costs or a wrong model.

8.5 A first year is not the long-run rate

A new portfolio pays every fee in its first weeks and receives its first payouts a month or more later; at the end of any finite horizon some accounts are part-way through and hold value not yet paid. So the first year's average cash, the average of later months, and the long-run renewal rate are three different quantities, and nothing requires them to be equal. Chapter [[CH_LOCK]] reports them separately: the first-year average from 12-month lives, and the average of months 13–36 and the value of the accounts still open at month 12 from 36-month lives. The months 13–36 average is a finite-window estimate: Chapter [[CH_LOCK]].6 compares its two annual windows with a paired interval before reading it as the long-run rate.
