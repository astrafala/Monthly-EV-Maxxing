Chapter 9

Optimising the settings

Four numbers define how the plan trades a programme: the risk per trade \(L\), the reward-to-risk \(k\), the stop \(m\) and the funded payout target \(X\); a fifth choice is the market. This chapter explains what each does, how they were searched, and what the search can and cannot claim.

9.2 The search

  1. Grid. For every programme and every market it may trade, every combination of \(L\), \(m\) (up to four stops from the leverage floor up), \(k \in \{1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12\}\) and five or six payout targets: [[N_GRID]] settings, each 2,000 attempts on four paths (101–104): [[N_GRID_ATTEMPTS]] simulated attempts.
  2. Refine. For each programme and market, the fourteen best grid settings, plus \(k\) half-way to its neighbours around the best (or 16 and 1.2 where the best sat at the end of the \(k\) grid) and a payout target 5 points larger, at most 30%, where the best sat at the top of its grid (not at Maven, whose grid ends at the plan's $4,900 ceiling), re-run for 16,000 attempts on four different paths (111–114), plus FTMO's best grid settings inside its 1.5% ceiling: [[N_REFINE]] settings, [[N_REFINE_ATTEMPTS]] attempts.
  3. Choose. The highest refined value per month with a stop of at least 0.6 hourly moves, a payout target of at most 30% of the account and, at FTMO, a risk of at most 1.5% (its own guidance, 10.5).
  4. Report. The chosen setting is run a third time on sixteen fresh paths (121–136), 24,000 attempts in all, with the cluster-robust interval of 8.4, and as a continuing slot on sixteen more (151–166, 8.3). A setting's figures from these runs are not inflated by having been picked as the best of many noisy estimates of that setting. The inflation is visible: the chosen settings average [[CURSE]] less on the fresh paths than on the paths that chose them. The continuing figures are then used again, to choose between a firm's programmes, markets and sizes (Part III); the sum of the chosen programmes' rates is therefore not a selection-free estimate, while the portfolio simulations, which run on new paths, are.

What the search establishes. The best setting found in this grid, for these programmes and these markets, under the model. It is not a proof of a global optimum over every possible rule, product, size or market; Part II lists what was searched for each programme and what was left out.

Chapter 10

Position size, leverage and margin

10.1 From risk to lots

The stop distance in price points is \(m\sigma\times\text{price}\). If one lot gains or loses \(V\) dollars per point (the firm's contract specification), then

Lots for a given risk $$\text{lots} = \frac{l}{m\,\sigma\,\text{price}\,V}, \qquad \text{notional} = \frac{l}{m\sigma}.$$

Lots are rounded down to the lot step, so the realised risk is never above \(l\), and never below the smallest volume. Since version 9 the engine trades exactly this (pathfirm.LOTS): index CFDs one index unit a lot ($1 a point; FTMO's MT5 contract size for its index CFDs is 1), gold 100 ounces a lot, currency pairs 100,000 units a lot, CFD volumes from 0.01 lot in steps of 0.01, at each market's level of 2 October 2026 scaled by the path (version 8 traded the exact notional \(l/s\) on CFDs, however small: review of version 8, finding 4). Other brokers' contract sizes change only the rounding. A win set by a cap keeps its amount at the rounded volume (its take-profit is placed for that win); an uncapped win \(k\,l\) scales with the rounded risk. The smallest position's risk, 0.01 lot at the plan's stop, is about $0.85 on the Nasdaq at \(m = 1\), $12 on gold and about $1 on the currency pairs, small next to a normal risk; it matters for the last trade near the floor (5.4) and for filler trades (6.3). Micro Nasdaq futures trade in whole contracts of $2 per point. The synthetic Nasdaq path starts at 100, so at every entry the engine converts its price to the index level, \(P_\text{index} = 31{,}070 \times P/100\), values one contract's stop at \(2\,P_\text{index}\,m\sigma\) dollars, and rounds the number of contracts down (never below one). Version 7 used 31,070 at every entry: at a path level of 125 that understated one contract's risk by a fifth. A futures account whose room is below one contract's stop makes its terminal trade with one contract, its stop order at the floor (5.4). Where one contract at the planned stop risks more than the planned \(l\) (on a path whose index has risen several-fold), the plan trades one contract if the floor and daily rooms allow it; the first version 8 engine waited instead, and those accounts never traded again (Appendix A, n11).

10.2 Leverage and margin, at every entry

A position of notional \(N\) needs margin \(N/\lambda\) at leverage 1:\(\lambda\). The plan keeps margin at most 60% of the current balance at every entry (version 6 checked it on the starting balance only):

Margin condition at entry, and the smallest stop at the start $$\frac{l}{m\sigma\lambda} \le 0.6\,(S + x) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad l \le 0.6\,\lambda\, m\sigma\,(S+x), \qquad m_{\min} = \frac{L/S}{0.6\,\lambda\,\sigma}.$$

The engine applies the first form to every trade, lowering the risk when the balance has fallen; the search starts its stops at \(m_{\min}\). Where a firm's leverage differs between evaluation and funded accounts (GFT: 1:20 then 1:10 on indices), the funded leverage sets \(m_{\min}\). FunderPro's funded accounts have a rule of their own: at most 20% of the starting balance as margin per asset class. At 1:30 on the Nasdaq that needs \(m \ge L/(S \times 0.2 \times 30 \times \sigma)\): 1.066 at the plan's 1.75% risk (0.913 at 1.5%; version 7's rule sheet quoted 0.92, the 1.5% figure, next to a 1.75% risk). GFT's funded 1:10 with the plan's 60% cap gives the same 1.066 at 1.75%. Version 6's FunderPro setting (\(m = 0.35\)) used about 52% and broke the rule.

10.3 Which market each account trades

  • Nasdaq 100 (US100 CFD, or micro Nasdaq futures): every firm that allows the position and the same trades as your own accounts elsewhere.
  • USDJPY: FundingPips (leverage), and any firm whose Nasdaq stop the leverage pushes too wide, where the search found it better.
  • Markets of their own for the firms that forbid copying from other firms' accounts or into funded accounts: FundedNext and Hola Prime each trade a market (EURUSD, gold) that no other account trades.

Inside each group every account takes the same direction (Chapter [[CH_LOCK]]), so no two accounts are ever on opposite sides of one market.

10.4 The ten-minute rule

FundingPips groups positions on one market and direction into one "trade idea" limited to 2% on funded accounts above $25K; Hola Prime treats a position opened within 10 minutes of closing one in the same direction and market, profitable or not, as the same trade idea, whose combined risk may not exceed 2%. The plan waits at least 10 minutes after every close before the next entry on that account, at every firm. Version 7 said that an entry at a later hour's open already met this; a close at 12:59 and an entry at 13:00 does not. The engine now knows each exit's minute from the one-minute sub-steps (3.3) and, after an exit later than minute 50, waits one more hour; tests_v9.py measures the shortest time from an exit to the next entry over thousands of consecutive trades ([[GAP_RESULT]]).

10.5 Risk per trade

Version 6 fixed the risk at 1.5% and argued from time alone. Under the 40% cap a larger risk lowers the effective reward:risk of a capped win, so the risk now matters for value too, and it is searched (1.0%, 1.5%, 1.75%). The upper end is 1.75% because Alpha forbids risking or losing 2% or more on a trade, FundingPips closes a trade idea at 2%, Hola Prime and FunderPro cap funded risk at 2%, and Blue Guardian's shield and GFT's Goat Guard act at a 2% open loss: 1.75% plus cost stays below all of them. FTMO publishes a recommendation of 1–1.5% of the initial balance per trade and says larger risk can trigger its risk monitoring; it is not a written rule, but the plan treats it as one and caps FTMO at 1.5%.

Chapter 11

Checking the engine against the formulas

The engine and the chain were written separately. The chain leaves out daily limits, minimum and profitable days, filler trades, holding times and duration rules, lot rounding, the per-day part of the concentration policy, payout dates (it pays only at the payout target, while the plan withdraws whatever profit there is on each payout date), the daily flat, weekend closes, the 10-minute gap and Maven's 30-day window; the engine includes them. Both include the terminal rule of 5.4 (the floor as the trade's barrier), the minimum target distance of 4.1 and, at Maven, the same stopping set: a cycle is paid at its target or within $1 of the $4,900 ceiling, and a win too small for the minimum distance is placed with its risk lowered (version 8's chain paid wherever the distance to the ceiling was below the smallest allowed win: review of version 8, finding 17). One effect exists only in the engine and only if positions are held over a weekend: a gap through the floor is absorbed by the firm, a gap through the target is kept. The plan is flat at weekends, so it does not arise; the verification stays flat at weekends too. Agreement where both apply is supporting evidence that each implements its model as described. It is not proof that either is right: two programs can agree because they share a wrong rule input or a wrong assumption. Where they differ, the difference should come from what the chain leaves out, and the third column of 11.2 measures exactly that, by running the engine with those rules switched off.
