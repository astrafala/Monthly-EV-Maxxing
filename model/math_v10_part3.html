One person, many accounts

Part II valued one account at a time, by its continuing-slot rate (8.3). [[CH_ALLOC]].2 Four 100K accounts or two 200K?

Scale every dollar amount of a programme by \(\lambda\) (account, targets, limits, risk, payout target, safety margins). The engine's rules then scale exactly: every trade's win, loss and cost scale by \(\lambda\), the pass probabilities and the time are unchanged, and the funded account's trading cash scales by \(\lambda\). The fee need not scale, and the refund follows the fee actually paid. Writing \(G(S)\) for the funded cash excluding the refund, \(R(S)\) for the refund and \(q_R\) for the chance of reaching the payout that carries it (given a pass):

Scaling an account (rules that scale) $$\mathbb{E}[V(\lambda S)] = P\big[\lambda\, G(S) + q_R\, R(\lambda S)\big] - F(\lambda S), \qquad \mathbb{E}[T](\lambda S) = \mathbb{E}[T](S).$$

Where the refund is the fee actually paid, less the firm's payout fee \(f\) (7.4; \(f = 0\) at most firms), \(R = (1-f)F\), and one account of size \(\lambda S\) is worth more than \(\lambda\) accounts of size \(S\) by \(\big[\lambda F(S) - F(\lambda S)\big]\big(1 - (1-f)\,P\,q_R\big)\) per attempt: the fee saving, less the part of it that would have come back as a refund; where no refund is paid, by the whole saving. (Version 6's formula scaled the refund with the account; it does not.) The formula does not apply where a rule is a fixed dollar amount: GFT's $3,000 day cap and the $10,000 limit in its first-payout cap min(6%, $10,000), The5ers' payout caps ($4,000 at 100K, $3,000 at 50K), Maven's $5,000 threshold (the plan's $4,900 ceiling), $10,000 rolling cap and $20 fee a payout, Hola Prime's $5 minimum payout fee, the fixed minimum payouts ($20 to $500), and whole futures contracts; and since version 9 it holds only up to rounding, because volumes are whole 0.01-lot steps and a filler or keep-alive position is 0.01 lot at any size. Chapter 11 measures the scaling path by path on the same seeds, once with the 200K fee set to twice the 100K fee (the rules) and once with the real fee (the contracts).

[[CH_ALLOC]].3 What the plan leaves out

The headline counts only what is modelled. Each of the following could add to it, and each is left out for a stated reason:

  • Discounts. The CFD figures use list prices, though several firms sell at a discount for much of the year (Blue Guardian's list prices have a 35% code, FTMO sold its 100K at EUR 439 in October 2026, FunderPro's is often 20% off and Maven's $440 is $396 with a coupon: Appendix [[APP_INV]]). A lower fee \(F'\) raises an account's value per attempt by \((F - F')\big(1 - (1-f)P q_R\big)\) where the refund is the fee paid, the saving less the part that would have come back as a refund ([[CH_ALLOC]].2), and by the whole saving where no refund is paid. Codes come and go, so none is assumed (the futures layer's Apex figures do assume one, as Appendix [[APP_INV]] states).
  • Scaling. FTMO (+25% every four months with 10% profit and two rewards), FXIFY and The5ers raise an account's size after sustained profit. The model keeps the purchased size, which forgoes any gain from scaling, and at The5ers the plan stays below the first milestone on purpose, because the scaled account's terms (timing, rules, the payout cap) are not fully published.
  • More accounts. FTMO allows any number of Challenges and caps funded accounts at $400,000 per person; the plan keeps four slots of one account each, because what happens when more Challenges pass than the cap allows is not published. FundedNext could hold a second funded account on a market of its own (within its $300,000), and Maven a second 100K account sharing the trader's $10,000 per 30 days; the model does neither. GFT, FunderPro, BrightFunded and Blue Guardian allow a trader's own accounts to trade alike, so their full allowances are modelled (Chapter [[CH_ALLOC]]); the recommended plan holds one account at each, as at every tier-D firm.
  • Rewards the plan does not count. FundedNext pays 15% of both phases' profit targets once its Scale-Up criteria are met, BrightFunded adds a 15% evaluation profit reward to the next funded account after 10% growth, and Alpha Capital may pay a discretionary bonus of 0.25% of the account with the fourth request; the plan does not scale and leaves all three out, so its figures understate those firms slightly.
  • Add-ons. Options sold at checkout, such as GFT's 100% split, are not modelled: each changes the price and the rules together and would need its own search.
Chapter [[CH_LOCK]]

Running every account at once

[[CH_LOCK]].1 What adds up and what does not

By linearity (2.1), the expected cash of a set of slots over a horizon is the sum of their expected cash over that horizon, under the policy actually run, however correlated the accounts are. Correlation changes the spread: in a bad week every Nasdaq account loses together, fees fall due together, and a finite budget can run short together.

[[CH_LOCK]].2 One direction per market

No two of your accounts ever hold opposite positions on one market at the same time, and EURUSD and USDJPY count as one market in dollars (a long EURUSD goes with a short USDJPY: 10.3). When a new trade opens and another of your accounts already holds that market, the new trade takes the same direction; when none does, the plan's rule decides (the sign of the move over the last five hourly closes). The rule has no edge (Chapter 11); its job is to keep every account on one side.

[[CH_LOCK]].3 The lockstep simulation

The portfolio results come from an engine that runs every account of one person on shared price paths and one calendar (lockstep.py). Every event, a trade's entry or a purchase, is processed in the order of its time; a position counts as open until its actual exit (the end of its exit minute), not until its account may trade again, so the shared-direction rule copies only positions really held (version 8 kept a closed position "open" for up to an hour longer: review of version 8, finding 11). Every firm's rules (with the plan's policies) apply to its own accounts, and every fee, credit, phase result and payout is written to a dated ledger. Every account keeps its firm's news blocks (6.5) and payout states (6.6), as in Part II. The settings were frozen before any portfolio run, and each simulated life runs on its own new paths (path numbers 500,000 and up, each market its own stream, 3.3), used by nothing else; version 7's market offsets made one life's yen path another life's Nasdaq path, which version 8 removed. Lives are therefore independent and their average is an unbiased estimate with an ordinary interval. [[CONFLICT_SENTENCE]]

[[CH_LOCK]].7 Finite cash, higher costs, correlation and behaviour

A finite budget. The first-year figures assume every slot is refilled the moment its attempt ends, which needs cash for fees before payouts arrive. Version 6 quoted the median of the deepest cash shortfall as if it kept every slot filled; half of all years need more. Here the plan is run with a fixed starting budget instead. A purchase is an event at its own time: it is paid from the cash dated at or before it (the starting cash, less earlier purchases, plus payouts received by then), and otherwise waits a day at a time. Version 8 let a purchase made while processing a later event use a payout dated after an earlier purchase, so five of its 1,400 budget lives went below zero on the dated ledger while its wallet reported a positive minimum (review of version 8, finding 1); every budget life now asserts that its dated cash never falls below zero.

Chapter [[CH_REF]]

If payouts can be refused

Every firm keeps the right to refuse a payout it judges to break its rules. Nobody publishes how often it happens. This chapter turns that uncertainty into scenarios; it does not cover payouts lost because a firm judges the strategy itself to break a behavioural rule (Chapter [[CH_RISK]].2), which would not be random.

[[CH_REF]].1 A model

Suppose each payout request at a firm is refused with probability \(\phi\), independently, and a refusal ends your use of that firm. The number of paid requests before the first refusal is geometric, with mean \((1-\phi)/\phi\). If the firm's requests arrive as a Poisson process at rate \(r\) a month (an assumption made for this illustration only), each of gross size \(a\), the expected cash actually paid over \(H\) months is

Paid cash under refusals $$a\,r\,(1-\phi)\,\frac{1 - e^{-\phi r H}}{\phi r} \;=\; a\,r\,H \times (1-\phi)\,\frac{1 - e^{-\phi r H}}{\phi r H},$$

the all-paid cash \(arH\) times the average survival fraction and the factor \((1-\phi)\): the refused request itself is not paid. For \(n\) requests on a fixed schedule instead, the expected paid amount is \(a\,(1-\phi)\,\big[1-(1-\phi)^n\big]/\phi\). Neither formula is used for the figures below: the simulation draws each refusal inside the event engine, at the moment the payout is requested, and stops using the firm from then on. Fees paid while the firm is still used count against the paid cash. [[REF_EXAMPLE]]
