Why it is lower than version 6's $138,412

Version 6 was reviewed in detail, and the review was right on almost every point (Appendix A). The largest changes are to the strategy itself, not to arithmetic. First, the plan no longer uses bold play: several firms forbid passing a phase with one or a few trades, so no trade and no day may make more than 40% of a target, and every phase and payout takes at least three winning days. Second, every position is closed before the daily resets, because several firms measure the daily limit on equity. What the number means, and what it does not

  • It is an expected value under a model: the average over many possible years if prices are fair random walks with the measured volatility and costs. The ± is the simulation's precision, not a range for real income.
  • It assumes zero skill: the value comes from the contract (a capped loss, a share of gains), not from predicting prices.
  • It assumes the firms pay as their rules say and do not judge the plan's trading to break their behavioural rules (Chapter 31.2). The refusal scenarios above price random refusals only.
  • It assumes every slot is refilled at once, which needs cash for fees; with a finite budget the first year is lower (Chapter 28.7).
  • It is per person: every firm limits what one person may hold. Accounts in anyone else's name are not allowed and are not in the plan.

How it was found

  1. Part I derives every formula and says which model it is exact for; the exact chain of 5.5 implements the plan's actual rule near the floor.
  2. Every programme's settings (risk, \(k\), stop, payout target, market) were searched over 10,941 grid settings and 401 refined ones (28 million simulated attempts), then re-measured on sixteen fresh paths each.
  3. The engine was checked against the chain with the daily rules switched off (Chapter 11), and the rules' own effect measured with them on.
  4. The accounts were run together, one person at a time, each simulated life on its own new paths: 8,400 first years, 800 three-year lives, and the budget and stress scenarios.

Part I

The mathematics, from zero

Every result in this document follows from a handful of facts about fair games. This part derives each of them, says exactly which model each formula is exact for, and works the numbers of the plan through at every step. Where a formula is an approximation it is labelled as one, and the engine (the rule-by-rule simulation) supplies the figure that is used.

Chapter 1

What is being bought

A proprietary trading firm ("prop firm") sells an evaluation. You pay a fee, you trade a simulated account under written rules, and if you meet the profit targets without breaking a loss limit, the firm gives you a funded account. On the funded account you keep a share of every profit you withdraw, and the firm absorbs every loss. You never owe the firm money beyond the fee.

That last sentence is the whole opportunity. Your downside is capped at the fee, and your upside is a share of profits that, as Chapter 7 shows, have a positive expected size even when every trade is a coin flip. The contract is an option: you pay a premium (the fee) for the right to keep part of the gains of a game whose losses fall on someone else.

1.2 The value of one attempt

Call the cash an attempt brings you \(V\). It is the fee (always paid), plus, if both phases are passed, whatever the funded account pays out (the refund included):

Value of one attempt $$V = -F + \mathbf{1}\{\text{pass}\}\cdot C, \qquad \mathbb{E}[V] = -F + \Pr(\text{pass})\cdot \mathbb{E}[C \mid \text{pass}]$$

Here \(\mathbf{1}\{\text{pass}\}\) is 1 if both phases are passed and 0 otherwise, and \(C\) is the total cash from the funded account. Where a firm returns part of the fee as non-cash credit when a phase is passed (The5ers), that credit is a third term, counted separately from cash (Chapter 14).

1.3 The whole argument in five lines, in an idealised model

Before the details, the result in its simplest form: no trading costs, every phase ending exactly at its target or its floor, and payouts of a fixed cycle target \(X\) from a static allowance. Chapter 5 proves that a fair game reaches \(+A\) before \(-B\) with probability \(B/(A+B)\) in that model; Chapter 7 proves that a funded account then withdraws on average exactly \(D\) before it is lost, and reaches its first payout with probability \(q = D/(D+X)\). The refund comes only with that first payout, so it is weighted by \(q\):

FTMO 2-Step, zero cost, zero skill, idealised $$\Pr(\text{pass}) = \frac{B}{A_1+B}\cdot\frac{B}{A_2+B} = \frac{10{,}000}{20{,}000}\cdot\frac{10{,}000}{15{,}000} = \frac{1}{3}$$ $$\mathbb{E}[V] = -F + \Pr(\text{pass})\big(\alpha D + q\,R\big) = -632 + \tfrac13\Big(8{,}000 + \tfrac{10{,}000}{10{,}000 + X}\,632\Big)$$

At the version 6 fee of $632 this is $2,140.00 with a cycle of \(X = \$10{,}000\) and $2,087.33 with \(X = \$30{,}000\); version 6 printed $2,245, which counts the refund on every pass and is only the limit as \(X \to 0\). At the correct fee of $607.82 and \(X = \$10{,}000\) it is \(-607.82 + \tfrac13(8{,}000 + 303.91) = \$2{,}160.15\).

Everything else in this document refines this number: trading costs lower it; the plan's policies (flat every evening, at most 40% of a target from one trade or one day, an attempt closed before it reaches the floor in micro size) change both the value and the time; and the settings are then chosen to make the most of it per month and per person. The sign is already visible here; its size needs the full engine.

1.4 Why the firm still makes money, and what that implies

The firm's revenue is the fees of everyone, and most traders are not zero-skill traders with a plan built around the contract: they over-trade, break rules, abandon accounts and stop after one failure. A plan that treats the contract as a mathematical object and repeats it many times is the case the firm's pricing does not cover. Firms know this, and their terms contain behavioural rules (against "gambling", "all-in" trading, one-trade target attainment, account rolling) that are written broadly and applied at their discretion. The plan is built to meet every rule that can be stated as a condition on trades, and Chapter 31 lists the behavioural rules that cannot, firm by firm, as conditions under which the figures here apply.

Chapter 2

The probability toolkit

Seven ideas carry the analysis. Each is stated with the conditions it needs.

2.1 Expected value and linearity

A random quantity \(X\) that takes values \(x_1, x_2, \dots\) with probabilities \(p_1, p_2, \dots\) has expected value \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_i p_i\, x_i\). For any random quantities, dependent or not,

$$\mathbb{E}[X+Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y], \qquad \mathbb{E}[cX] = c\,\mathbb{E}[X].$$

Linearity says that the expected cash of many accounts over one horizon, under one policy is the sum of their expected cash over that horizon, however strongly they move together. It does not say that a first year's average equals a long-run rate (they are different quantities, Chapter 8.5), and it does not make correlation irrelevant: correlation leaves the sum of the means alone and changes everything else (the spread, the cash needed for fees, the chance of many failures at once).

2.2 Variance

\(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2\). For independent \(X, Y\) variances add; for dependent ones a term \(2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\) is added. Variance enters the time a fair game takes (Chapter 8) and the confidence intervals of every simulated figure.

2.3 Fair games and martingales

A sequence of balances \(X_0, X_1, \dots\) is a martingale if \(\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid X_0,\dots,X_n] = X_n\). "Zero skill" means this for the balance before costs: the price is a martingale, the balance is a sum of positions times price changes, and no rule for direction, size or timing that uses only the past can change that. With costs the expected next balance is lower by the cost: a supermartingale.

2.4 Stopping times and the optional stopping theorem

A stopping time \(\tau\) is a rule for when to stop that uses only information available at the time. The optional stopping theorem: if \(X\) is a martingale, \(\tau\) is almost surely finite, and the stopped process \(X_{n\wedge\tau}\) is bounded (or, more generally, uniformly integrable), then

$$\mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = X_0 .$$

Each use below states why the condition holds. For one phase it holds because the balance stays between the floor and the target plus one win. For a funded account whose profits are withdrawn the balance plus everything withdrawn is not bounded, so Chapter 7 applies the theorem to one payout cycle at a time, where it is.

2.5 Wald's identity

If each step adds an amount with mean \(\mu\) and \(\tau\) is a stopping time with \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] < \infty\), then \(\mathbb{E}[X_\tau - X_0] = \mu\,\mathbb{E}[\tau]\). With costs, every trade's mean is minus its own cost (4.3), so the identity becomes \(\mathbb{E}[X_\tau - X_0] = -\mathbb{E}[\text{total cost}]\), which holds even when the cost varies from trade to trade.

2.6 Brownian motion

The continuous-time limit of a fair random walk is Brownian motion \(W_t\): it starts at 0, has independent increments, and \(W_{t+h} - W_t\) is normal with mean 0 and variance \(h\). A process \(X_t = \mu t + \sigma W_t\) has three martingales that carry the approximations of Chapters 5 and 8:

$$X_t - \mu t, \qquad (X_t-\mu t)^2 - \sigma^2 t, \qquad \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{2\mu}{\sigma^2} X_t\right).$$

The third follows from Itô's formula: \(d e^{\lambda X} = e^{\lambda X}(\lambda\mu + \tfrac12\lambda^2\sigma^2)\,dt + (\dots)\,dW\), whose \(dt\) term vanishes for \(\lambda = -2\mu/\sigma^2\). These formulas are exact for Brownian motion; applied to a sum of bracket trades, which jump, they are approximations (5.2).

2.7 The reflection principle

For a Brownian path from \(a\) to \(b\) over a time with variance \(v\), the chance that it rose above \(y \ge \max(a,b)\) in between is

$$\Pr\big(\max \ge y \,\big|\, a \to b\big) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{2(y-a)(y-b)}{v}\right).$$

This gives the marginal distribution of the maximum of a bridge (and, by symmetry, of the minimum). It does not by itself say which of a high and a low level was touched first. Chapter 3.3 describes how the simulation orders the two, and Chapter 11 measures what is left of the error.

3.2 Zero drift, stated precisely

The model gives the price no expected change: \(\mathbb{E}[P_{t+h} \mid P_t] = P_t\). That is the zero-skill assumption. For the price itself to be a martingale, the log price must drift down by \(\sigma^2/2\) per hour (about 0.0004% for the Nasdaq): \(\log P_{t+h} - \log P_t\) is normal with mean \(-\sigma^2 h/2\) and variance \(\sigma^2 h\). Version 6 said the log price had mean zero; the construction in 3.3 always included the correction, and the statement is now made about the price. The Nasdaq's real long-run rise (about 0.0025% per hour, a hundredth of \(\sigma\)) is left out: the plan is long and short equally often, so it would neither help nor hurt.

3.3 Synthetic paths

  1. Each hour is split into 12 sub-steps of normal increments of the log price with variance \(\sigma^2/12\) and mean \(-\sigma^2/24\).
  2. Within each sub-step, the highest and the lowest point are drawn from the exact marginal distributions of a Brownian bridge between the sub-step's end points (2.7): $$\max = \tfrac12\left(a+b+\sqrt{(b-a)^2 - 2v\ln U}\right),\qquad U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1),$$ and the minimum is the mirror image with an independent draw.
  3. When a stop and a target both fall inside one hourly bar, the engine replays that hour's twelve sub-steps in time order and takes the level the path touched first. The order is therefore exact unless both levels fall inside the same five-minute sub-step; only then does it fall back to the trade's overall fair chance, which is not the right conditional probability. Chapter 11 measures the remaining effect at 12 and at 60 sub-steps.
  4. Markets that close at weekends are closed in the path from Friday to Sunday, but the price keeps moving underneath, so Monday can open with a gap. Under the plan's daily flat (6.5) no position is open then.
  5. Every path is fixed by its seed. Each stage of the work uses its own seeds, never reused: the grid search paths 101–104, the refinement 111–114, the final value of every chosen setting 121–136 (sixteen independent paths), the sensitivities 141–144, the checks 401–444, and the portfolio simulation one new path per simulated life (seeds 100,000 and up). Gold, USDJPY and EURUSD use their own paths (seed + 50, + 60, + 70), independent of the Nasdaq; Chapter 28 measures what a correlation of 0.3 between all four markets changes.

3.4 What the model leaves out

Real prices have volatility that changes (calm and wild months), fat tails, jumps at news, and spreads that widen at the daily break and around releases. The plan's stops are at least 0.35 hourly standard deviations so that average costs are a fair description, and the cost stress tests of Chapter 31 double every cost. The synthetic model is the right tool for the question asked (what is the contract worth to a trader with no edge), not a forecast of any particular year.

Chapter 4

The bracket trade

Every trade in the plan is a bracket: one position with a stop-loss and a take-profit attached the moment it opens, never moved, and closed at the latest at 20:00 UTC the same day.

4.1 Set-up

Open a long position at price \(e\) with notional \(N\) dollars (a short is the mirror image), stop a fraction \(s\) below the entry and target a fraction \(u\) above. The plan fixes three things and derives the rest:

  • the risk \(l\): what a stopped trade loses before costs. Normally \(l = L\) (1–1.75% of the account, searched in Chapter 9), but never more than the room left, net of the trade's own cost (5.4);
  • the stop distance \(s = m\sigma\): \(m\) hourly standard deviations;
  • the win \(w\) the trade nets after costs if it reaches its target: \(w = \min(k\,l,\ \text{distance to the target},\ \text{caps})\), where \(k\) is the reward-to-risk setting and the caps are the plan's 40% concentration policy (6.7) and any firm cap.
Position and target $$N = \frac{l}{s}, \qquad N u - c = w \;\Rightarrow\; u = \frac{s\,(w+c)}{l}.$$

A win pays \(+w\) and a loss costs \(-(l+c)\), where \(c = \kappa N\) is the round-trip cost (spread, commission, slippage allowance) of this position.

4.2 The win probability

The price is a martingale and the trade ends at the first touch of the stop or the target. If nothing else can end it and fills are exact, the exit price is bounded, so optional stopping (2.4) gives \(p\,e(1+u) + (1-p)\,e(1-s) = e\), i.e. \(p = s/(s+u)\):

Win probability of a zero-skill bracket (fixed cost, exact fills) $$p = \frac{l}{l + w + c} = \frac{1}{1 + w/l + c/l}.$$

4.3 The mean and the variance of one trade

With outcomes \(+w\) (probability \(p\)) and \(-(l+c)\):

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{pnl}] = p\,w - (1-p)(l+c) = \frac{l\,w - (w+c)(l+c)}{l+w+c} = -c,$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(\text{pnl}) = p\,(w+c)^2 + (1-p)\,l^2 = l\,(w+c).$$

These hold for the binary trade of 4.2 with a cost known at entry. Two things in the plan make some outcomes non-binary. A trade still open at 20:00 UTC is closed at that hour's price, somewhere between stop and target; the price is still a martingale and the closing time a bounded stopping time, so the mean is still \(-c\), but the variance is smaller and the distribution has three parts. And a price that gaps through a level fills at the gap. The engine simulates both; the chain of Chapter 5 uses the binary trade.

4.6 Ways a simulation can flatter a trade

  1. Skipping the entry bar. Checking levels only from the next hour ignores stop-outs inside the entry hour. (Measured in version 4: +0.14 R per trade, all fake.)
  2. Sampling prices too rarely. A few prices an hour miss touches in between, and the nearer level (the stop) is missed more often. The bridge extremes of 3.3 remove it.
  3. Ties inside one bar. When one bar's range contains both levels, the bar alone does not say which came first. Version 5 used the fair chance; version 6 replays the sub-steps (3.3). The remaining tie inside one five-minute step is measured in Chapter 11 at 12 and 60 sub-steps.
  4. Gaps. A price that opens beyond the stop fills at the open. Under the daily flat there is no overnight or weekend position to gap.
  5. A rounding error at the target. A win sized to close the gap to the target lands a few billionths of a dollar short in floating point; version 6's exact comparison then placed a pointless extra trade (risking $1,500 to make $1). Version 7 compares with a one-cent tolerance.
Chapter 5

Reaching a target before a floor

A phase is passed when the balance gains \(A\) before it loses \(B\): the gambler's ruin. This chapter solves it in an idealised model, then exactly for the plan's actual rule.

5.1 The idealised model: \(P = B/(A+B)\)

Let \(X_n\) be the balance after \(n\) trades, a martingale with \(X_0 = 0\), and \(\tau\) the first time it reaches \(+A\) or \(-B\). If every trade is sized so the balance lands exactly on one of the two, \(X_\tau\) is \(+A\) with probability \(P\) and \(-B\) otherwise, and optional stopping gives

$$0 = \mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = P\,A - (1-P)\,B \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; P = \frac{B}{A+B}.$$

The exact-landing assumption is what this formula needs, and Section 5.4 shows that the plan's rule does not land exactly on the floor (it stops a little above it). The formula is the benchmark; 5.5 gives the exact value for the actual rule.

5.2 With costs, approximately: the Brownian formula

Treat the balance as a Brownian motion with drift \(-c\) and variance \(v = l(w+c)\) per trade. By 2.6, \(e^{\theta X}\) with \(\theta = 2c/v\) is a martingale, and optional stopping gives

Pass probability with costs (Brownian approximation) $$P \approx \frac{e^{\theta B} - 1}{e^{\theta (A+B)} - 1}, \qquad \theta = \frac{2c}{v} = \frac{2\rho}{l\,(w/l + \rho)},\qquad P \approx \frac{B}{A+B}\left(1 - \frac{c\,A}{v}\right).$$

This is accurate when each trade is small next to \(A\) and \(B\) and its size does not change. The plan's trades are capped (40% of the target), shrink near the floor and the daily limit, and jump: the exact chain of 5.5 is used instead, and the Brownian value is shown only for comparison.

5.3 How many trades, approximately

With a constant trade, \((X_n)^2 - n v\) is a martingale before costs, so \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] = \mathbb{E}[X_\tau^2]/v = AB/v\) in the idealised model. With trades that change size the relevant quantity is the accumulated variance \(\sum_n v(X_n)\), not \(v\) times the count; the chain computes the expected count exactly.

5.4 The plan's rule near the floor

Version 6's written rule (risk = the room above the floor less a $100 buffer; wait for tomorrow when that is below $150) could leave an account a few dollars above its static floor with no trade ever allowed, and its engine did something different again (it traded micro positions of the remaining room). Version 7 has one rule, the same in the text, the engine and the chain. At a balance \(x\) (relative to the start), with floor \(-B\), buffer \(b\) ($100 per $100K) and the cost per dollar of risk \(\rho\):

Risk of the next trade, and when the attempt ends $$\text{room}(x) = \frac{x + B - b}{1+\rho}, \qquad l(x) = \min\big(L,\ \text{room}(x),\ \text{today's room},\ \text{margin room}\big),$$ $$\text{the attempt ends at } x \text{ (a failure) if } \text{room}(x) < l_{\min} = \max(0.1\,L,\ \text{one futures contract}).$$

A stopped trade then loses \(l(1+\rho) \le x + B - b\), so the balance never reaches the floor through a stop: every failure is an abandonment at a balance between \(-B + b\) and \(-B + b + l_{\min}(1+\rho)\). In practice: the account is no longer traded, the replacement is bought, and the old account lapses. Nothing in any firm's rules obliges a trader to trade an account down to its floor. (When only today's room is too small, the account waits for tomorrow; today's room resets, the static room does not.)

5.5 The exact chain

The balance after each trade is a Markov chain on \([-B+b, A]\). At \(x\) the next trade has risk \(l(x)\) as above, win \(w(x) = \max(\min(k\,l(x),\ A - x,\ \text{cap}),\ \$1)\), cost \(c(x) = \rho\,l(x)\) and win chance \(p(x) = l/(l + w + c)\), and moves to \(x + w\) or \(x - l - c\). Four functions solve the same recursion with different end values:

One step of the exact chain $$V(x) = p\,V(x+w) + (1-p)\,V(x-l-c), \qquad V = 1 \text{ at } A,\ 0 \text{ where the attempt ends},$$ $$N(x) = 1 + p\,N(x+w) + (1-p)\,N(x-l-c), \qquad K(x) = c(x) + p\,K(x+w) + (1-p)\,K(x-l-c),$$ $$T(x) = p\,T(x+w) + (1-p)\,T(x-l-c), \qquad T = \text{the balance where the attempt ends}.$$

\(V\) is the pass probability, \(N\) the expected number of trades, \(K\) the expected total cost and \(T\) the expected final balance. They are solved by value iteration on a grid of about 8,000 points (analytic_v7.py). Because every trade's mean is exactly minus its own cost (4.3) and the stopped balance is bounded, optional stopping gives the general accounting identity, which the solver checks:

The general accounting identity, exact $$P\,A + (1-P)\,\mathbb{E}[X_{\text{end}} \mid \text{fail}] = -K(0) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P = \frac{\mathbb{E}[-X_{\text{end}} \mid \text{fail}] - K(0)}{A + \mathbb{E}[-X_{\text{end}} \mid \text{fail}]}.$$

The textbook form \(P = (B - K)/(A+B)\) is the special case where every failure ends exactly at \(-B\). Under the plan's rule a failure ends about \(b\) above the floor, so \(\mathbb{E}[-X_\text{end} \mid \text{fail}]\) is a little less than \(B\) and the textbook form overstates \(P\) slightly (0.25% to 0.37% (percentage points) over the chosen programmes).

5.6 Bold play, and why the plan does not use it

Each trade costs \(c\) on average, so a phase's pass probability falls with its expected total cost (5.5). The way to pay the least cost is to need the fewest trades: stake enough to reach the target in one win. This is bold play, which Dubins and Savage (1965) proved optimal for reaching a goal in a fair or slightly unfavourable game. Version 6's search found it: left free, every programme moved to \(k\) of 20–30 and payout cycles of 25–30% of the account, so that a single trade could pass a phase or fill a payout.

Version 7 gives that up, deliberately. Several firms forbid exactly this behaviour: Fintokei forbids reaching a target "in one or a few identical trades", The5ers bans "one-trade target attainment" permanently, Alpha forbids "all or nothing" trading, FundingPips' Profit Concentration Policy reacts to one trade idea above 60% of a target, and FundedNext, Hola Prime, FTMO and FXIFY reserve the right to treat concentrated outcomes as gambling. The plan therefore caps every trade and every day at 40% of the current target (6.7), so that every phase and every payout takes at least three winning days. Under that cap one win can never end a phase, the product formula for bold play (the chance of passing as a sequence of all-or-nothing trades) never applies, and the chain of 5.5 is the exact value.

For reference, the bold product formula, \(P = \sum_j p_j \prod_{i<j}(1-p_i)\) with \(x_{j+1} = x_j - l_j - c_j\), is exact only if every possible win ends the phase, i.e. \(k\,l(x_j) \ge A - x_j\) at every state on the all-loss path; \(k L \ge A + B\) is not enough, because \(l(x)\) shrinks near the floor. Version 6's tables printed wins of \(A - x_j\) even where \(k\,l(x_j)\) was smaller; those rows described trades the plan would not place.

Chapter 6

The evaluation in full

6.1 Two phases

Always, \(\Pr(\text{pass both}) = P_1 \cdot \Pr(\text{pass 2} \mid \text{passed 1})\). In the simulation's model the second phase starts on a fresh account and its trades are independent of the first's, so the conditional probability equals the unconditional \(P_2\) and the product \(P_1P_2\) holds. On real markets a volatile month that carries from one phase into the next could link them; zero edge alone does not rule that out, and the product is a model statement, not a theorem about markets. The engine simulates the two phases in sequence on one path, so whatever its path carries over is included.

Version 6 also said that putting the larger target first saves time and cost. It does not in general: with the Brownian trade count of 5.3 and targets \(A, C\) over a common \(B\), the expected number of trades is \(AB/v + \frac{B}{A+B}\cdot CB/v\), which for \(A = 10{,}000\), \(C = 5{,}000\), \(B = 10{,}000\) and \(v = 1{,}500^2 \times 5\) is 11.11 trades, against 10.37 with the targets reversed. The claim is withdrawn.

6.3 Minimum trading days: the joint condition

FTMO (4), FundedNext (5), BrightFunded (5), FXIFY Classic (4) and Alpha (3) require days with at least one trade. A phase is passed when the target is met and the days are complete at the same time. If the target is reached first, the plan stops trading the strategy and places one minimal trade (smallest lot, held at least 2 minutes) on each remaining weekday. Each costs a few cents to a few dollars; to make sure they cannot take the balance back under the target, the target the engine trades to is the firm's target plus $20 per $100K. Filler days are weekdays (five in seven calendar days). This is not done where the rule counts profitable days (6.4).

6.4 Profitable-day rules

The5ers, Fintokei, Maven, Blue Guardian, GFT (whose days count only with +0.5%) and FundingPips' 95% option require days with at least +0.5% profit. While such days are missing, the plan trades normally until the day's profit reaches

$$g_{\text{today}} = \max\!\left(0.55\%\,S,\; \frac{\text{distance left to the target}}{\text{profitable days still needed}}\right)$$

and then stops for the day; every trade is a full fair bracket whose take-profit is capped at what is left of today's target. Stopping at a daily target is a stopping time, so in a cost-free fair game the pass probability is unchanged; with costs, every extra trade costs \(c\), so these rules lower the value as well as stretching the time. Version 6 said they change "only time"; that holds only without costs and is withdrawn. The engine measures their effect in every programme's chapter.

6.5 The daily flat, weekends and news

The plan opens no position from 19:00 UTC and closes every position at the 20:00 UTC open, every weekday. Every daily reset in the plan falls between 20:00 and 00:00 UTC (FTMO and BrightFunded near midnight Prague time, GFT and Blue Guardian at 5 pm New York, Fintokei and Maven at 00:00 UTC), so the account is flat at each of them. This also removes overnight financing, overnight and weekend gaps, and the widened spreads of the daily break. The cost is time: a trade still open at 20:00 is closed between its stop and its target (a fair outcome) and the next one waits until the next day's open.

News. Restricted news windows differ by firm (Maven: no opening or closing within 2 minutes of a red-folder release, in evaluations too; FundingPips: profits from 5 minutes before to 5 minutes after may be deducted; FTMO funded: no new orders within 2 minutes; FundedNext: 40% of news profit counted on funded accounts). A bracket opened earlier can still reach its stop or target inside a window. The plan closes positions before any release that a firm restricts for the account's stage and market (US, euro-area and Japanese releases alike) and opens nothing until the window has passed. The engine has no news calendar; Chapter 28 runs a bounding test in which every account is flat from 12:00 to 14:00 UTC (and 08:00 to 09:00 on EURUSD) every weekday.

6.6 Review time

Firms take one to three working days to check a passed phase and issue the next account. The engine adds one day after phase 1 and the firm's stated time after phase 2.

6.7 The concentration policy

On every account the plan caps the win of every trade at 40% of the current target (the phase target in an evaluation, the payout target on a funded account), and stops for the day once the day's profit reaches 40% of that target. Three consequences follow:

  • every phase and every payout needs at least three winning days, so no outcome rests on one trade or one day;
  • FundingPips' Profit Concentration Policy (60% of a phase target from one trade idea) can never trigger, and FXIFY's 25%, Hola Prime's 40% and Maven's 20% rules are met by their own tighter caps;
  • the effective reward-to-risk is capped: in phase 2 of a 5% target on 100K a win is at most $2,000, so at 1.5% risk at most 1.33 : 1. The search (Chapter 9) therefore covers smaller \(k\) and the risk per trade.

Chapter 9 measures what the policy costs against the uncapped strategy of version 6, programme by programme.

Chapter 7

What a funded account pays

On the funded account the plan trades each payout cycle to a profit target \(X\) above the starting balance, stops, requests the payout on the first allowed day, and withdraws everything above the start. The balance is back at the start, the static floor is still \(D\) below it, and the next cycle begins. If a payout date comes before the target, the plan withdraws whatever profit there is (where the firm's rules allow) and starts a new cycle; the cycle formulas below describe the simpler policy of paying only at the target, and the engine simulates the plan's. The account ends when it is abandoned near the floor under the rule of 5.4 (or, at a few firms, after a fixed number of payouts).

7.1 The withdrawal identity, with its conditions

Take one cycle. Its balance (relative to the start) is a stopped sum of trades, bounded between \(-D\) and \(X\) plus one win, so optional stopping applies to it: if cycle \(i\) ends with balance \(Z_i\) (either its target \(X_i\), withdrawn, or the abandonment level) after costs \(K_i\), then \(\mathbb{E}[Z_i] = -\mathbb{E}[K_i]\). The account's total withdrawal is \(W = \sum_i X_i\,\mathbf{1}\{\text{cycle } i \text{ paid}\}\) and it ends with balance \(X_{\text{end}}\) in its last cycle. Adding the cycles (the number of cycles has finite mean because each fails with a probability bounded away from zero):

The withdrawal identity, general form $$\mathbb{E}[W] = -\,\mathbb{E}[X_{\text{end}}] - \mathbb{E}[\text{total funded cost}].$$

Only when the account always ends exactly at the floor (\(X_\text{end} = -D\)) does this become \(\mathbb{E}[W] = D - \mathbb{E}[\text{cost}]\), the form version 6 stated for every policy. It changes when the account can end elsewhere: abandonment a little above the floor (version 7's rule: \(\mathbb{E}[-X_\text{end}]\) slightly below \(D\)), a daily-limit breach part-way down, profit forfeited (GFT's day cap, Maven's rolling cap), a closure after a fixed number of payouts (Apex), or a floor that moves (Topstep, Apex). Version 6 asserted the static identity for the trailing futures accounts; that was wrong, and those chapters now report only what the engine measures. Version 6's proof also appealed to boundedness of the balance plus withdrawals, which is not bounded; the cycle-by-cycle argument above is the correct one.

7.2 Cycle by cycle

Each cycle is a fresh run of the chain of 5.5 from 0 to its target \(X_i\) before the abandonment level, with success chance \(q_i\). Cycles continue until one fails, so cycle \(j\) is paid with probability \(\prod_{i\le j} q_i\). With a first cycle \(X_1\) and later ones \(X\) (chances \(q_1, q\)):

Funded cash, cycle form $$\mathbb{E}[C] = \alpha\left(X_1 q_1 + X\,\frac{q_1\,q}{1-q}\right) + R\cdot q_1 q^{\,r-1},$$

where the refund \(R\) comes with the \(r\)-th payout (\(r=1\) at most firms; FundingPips Standard and Blue Guardian 4, Maven 3); a refund in \(r\) equal parts with the first \(r\) payouts (Hola Prime) contributes \(\frac{R}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r} q_1 q^{\,j-1}\). With two special first cycles (GFT: the first two payouts are capped, so the first two cycles have target \(X_a\) and chance \(q_a\), later ones \(X_b\), \(q_b\)):

Two special first cycles (GFT) $$\mathbb{E}[\text{paid cycles}] = q_a + q_a^2 + \frac{q_a^2\,q_b}{1-q_b} = q_a + \frac{q_a^2}{1-q_b}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[W] = X_a\,(q_a + q_a^2) + X_b\,\frac{q_a^2\,q_b}{1-q_b}.$$

(Version 6 printed \(q_1/(1-q)\) for GFT, the one-special-cycle formula; its number came from the correct one.) In the zero-cost idealised model \(q = D/(X+D)\), \(\mathbb{E}[\text{paid cycles}] = D/X\) and \(\mathbb{E}[W] = D\), which is where the zero-cost example of 1.3 takes \(q\) from.

7.3 What the cycle target changes

In the idealised zero-cost model, nothing about the average. With costs, at fixed \(l\) and \(k\), the expected number of funded trades grows with \(X\): by 5.3 it is about \(XD/v\) per cycle times \((X+D)/X\) expected cycles, i.e. \(D(X+D)/v\). So a larger cycle means more trades and more cost over the account's life, and more time between payouts; a smaller one means more payout requests and more waiting for payout dates. Version 6 said larger cycles mean fewer trades and less cost; that contradicted this formula and is withdrawn. With caps, daily targets and payout calendars the balance between the two effects is found by search (Chapter 9), and the measured effect of \(X\) is shown in each programme's chapter.

7.4 Funded rules and what they change

  • Best-day and largest-trade rules. If no day may exceed a share \(\beta\) of a payout's profit, each day's take-profit is capped at \(\beta X\) less what the day has made (FXIFY 25%, Hola Prime 40%; the plan's own 40% everywhere). Maven requires the largest winning trade to be at most 20% of the payout's profit, so each win is capped at \(0.2X\) and a payout is requested only when the rule holds.
  • Profitable days per cycle (FundingPips 95%: 3; Hola Prime: 3; GFT: 4): the daily profit target of 6.4, applied per cycle.
  • Payout calendars. The first payout is allowed 14 to 30 days after the account opens, then every 10 to 30 days. A cycle that reaches its target early waits, flat.
  • Payout caps (GFT: the first two payouts at most 6% or $10,000): the first two cycles aim at the cap.
  • Daily profit caps (GFT: $3,000 a day at every size, excess deducted): the take-profit is capped at what is left of the day's cap.
  • Rolling caps (Maven: $10,000 per trader per rolling 30 days, excess voided): the plan requests a payout only when the window has room for all of it, and otherwise waits.

Without costs, rules that only cap or delay wins leave the average withdrawal unchanged; with costs, each extra trade costs \(c\), and rules that forfeit profit (day caps, rolling caps) change it directly. The engine includes all of them.

Time, and value per month

8.1 How long one trade lasts, approximately

For a driftless arithmetic Brownian motion with variance \(\sigma^2\) per hour, \(Z_t^2 - \sigma^2 t\) is a martingale, and stopping at \(+d_t\) or \(-d_s\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] = d_s d_t/\sigma^2\). For the bracket, \(d_s = m\sigma\) and \(d_t = m\sigma(w+c)/l\):

Expected duration of a bracket (hours of open market), approximation $$\mathbb{E}[\tau] \approx m^2\,\frac{w + c}{l}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\tau \mid \text{win}] \approx \frac{m^2}{3}\Big(\big(\tfrac{w+c}{l}\big)^2 + 2\,\tfrac{w+c}{l}\Big), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\tau \mid \text{loss}] \approx \frac{m^2}{3}\Big(1 + 2\,\tfrac{w+c}{l}\Big).$$

The synthetic price is geometric: its log has drift \(-\sigma^2/2\) per hour, and the barriers sit at log distances \(-\ln(1-s)\) and \(\ln(1+u)\), not \(s\) and \(u\). For the plan's brackets (\(s\) and \(u\) a fraction of a percent to a few percent) the difference is of the order of \(s\) and \(u\) themselves, a fraction of a percent of the duration. The daily flat also cuts long trades at 20:00 UTC. So these formulas are used only to explain orders of magnitude; every duration reported in Part II is measured by the engine.

8.2 How long a phase lasts

In the idealised model the number of trades is \(AB/v\) (5.3) with \(v = l(w+c)\), and each lasts about \(m^2(w+c)/l\) hours, so the market time is about \((A/l)(B/l)\,m^2\) hours, independent of \(k\). For FTMO phase 1 at \(m = 0.75\) and \(l = \$1{,}500\): \(6.67^2 \times 0.56 \approx 25\) hours of open market. The calendar is set by the rules instead: minimum and profitable days, the concentration policy's three winning days, the daily flat, weekends, reviews and payout dates.

8.3 Value per month: the renewal–reward theorem

The plan runs slots: one account position at a firm, refilled with a new evaluation as soon as an attempt ends. A slot completes attempts with values \(V_i\) and durations \(T_i\), independent and identically distributed in the model, and

Renewal–reward theorem $$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n V_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n T_i} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[V]}{\mathbb{E}[T]}, \qquad \text{EV per month} = 30.44\times\frac{\mathbb{E}[V]}{\mathbb{E}[T\ \text{in days}]}.$$

This is a long-run rate: the average over many attempts, the ratio of expectations (not the expectation of ratios). It is the objective each programme's settings are chosen for.

8.4 How precise a simulated figure is

With \(n\) independent attempts the ratio estimate \(\hat r = \bar V/\bar T\) has, by the delta method, standard error \(\operatorname{sd}(V_i - \hat r\,T_i)/(\sqrt{n}\,\bar T)\). Attempts that start at random times on the same synthetic path share its history, so they are not fully independent. Version 7's final figures therefore use 16 independent paths per programme (1,500 attempts on each) and treat each path as a cluster:

Cluster-robust interval for a ratio $$\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat r) = \frac{G}{G-1}\,\frac{\sum_{g=1}^{G}\big(V_g - \hat r\,T_g\big)^2}{\big(\sum_g T_g\big)^2},$$

where \(V_g, T_g\) are the totals on path \(g\). With only \(G\) clusters the multiplier for a 95% interval is the Student \(t\) quantile with \(G-1\) degrees of freedom, not 1.96: 2.13 for 16 paths, 3.18 for 4. Every "±" for a programme is the larger of that cluster interval and 1.96 attempt-level standard errors. The checks of Chapter 11 use 24 independent paths per programme for the same reason: with thousands of attempts on one path, the same stretch of price history is traded dozens of times, and an attempt-level interval would be several times too narrow. Portfolio lives each run on their own new path, so they are independent and the ordinary standard error applies. These are Monte Carlo intervals under the model: they say how well the model's expectation is estimated, not how much a real year can differ from it, and they carry no allowance for wrong rules, wrong costs or a wrong model.

8.5 A first year is not the long-run rate

A new portfolio pays every fee in its first weeks and receives its first payouts a month or more later; at the end of any finite horizon some accounts are part-way through and hold value not yet paid. So the first year's average cash, the average of later months, and the long-run renewal rate are three different quantities, and nothing requires them to be equal. Version 6 said linearity made its first-year figure and its sum of long-run rates "agree"; that was wrong. Chapter 28 reports them separately: the first-year average from 12-month lives, and the long-run monthly rate and the value of the accounts still open at month 12 from 36-month lives.

8.6 The plan's attempt, timed

Engine, sixteen fresh paths: an attempt lasts \(\mathbb{E}[T] = 15.35\) days on average, from the purchase to the failed phase or the closed funded account.

So one slot buys \(30.44/15.35 = 1.98\) evaluations a month and pays \(1.98\times\$607.82 = \$1{,}205\) a month in fees before refunds.

Value of an attempt: \(\mathbb{E}[V] = \$1{,}478\); pass rate 27.7%; cash per funded account $7,543.

Renewal–reward (8.3): \(30.44 \times 1{,}478 / 15.35 = \$2{,}930\) per month, ± $294 (95%, cluster-robust over 16 paths; attempt-level ± $170).

9.3 What the search found

The policy moves the optimum. Under the 40% cap no win can end a phase, so the version 6 corner (k of 20–30, one trade per phase) is gone. The chosen settings use risk 1.50% (2), 1.75% (22), \(k\) from 3 to 10, stops from 0.75 to 1.25 and payout targets from 10% to 30% of the account.

Why the risk goes to its upper limit. With wins capped at 40% of the target, a larger risk mainly makes the account move faster (market time \(\propto 1/L^2\), 8.2) at almost the same pass probability, so value per month rises with \(L\); 1.75% is the plan's ceiling, set by the firms' 2% rules, not by the search, and FTMO is held to its own recommended 1.5% (10.5).

Why the payout target goes to its upper limit. A larger target means fewer payout dates to wait for and, under the 40% cap, larger wins per trade; the cost of more funded trades (7.3) is smaller than the time saved. 30% is the plan's ceiling (one payout of more than 30% of the account invites review).

Chapter 10

Position size, leverage and margin

10.1 From risk to lots

The stop distance in price points is \(m\sigma\times\text{price}\). If one lot gains or loses \(V\) dollars per point (the firm's contract specification), then

Lots for a given risk $$\text{lots} = \frac{l}{m\,\sigma\,\text{price}\,V}, \qquad \text{notional} = \frac{l}{m\sigma}.$$

Lots are rounded down to the lot step, so the realised risk is never above \(l\). Micro Nasdaq futures trade in whole contracts of $2 per point: the engine rounds the number of contracts down and never trades fewer than one, and a futures account whose room is below one contract's stop is closed (5.4).

10.2 Leverage and margin, at every entry

A position of notional \(N\) needs margin \(N/\lambda\) at leverage 1:\(\lambda\). The plan keeps margin at most 60% of the current balance at every entry (version 6 checked it on the starting balance only):

Margin condition at entry, and the smallest stop at the start $$\frac{l}{m\sigma\lambda} \le 0.6\,(S + x) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad l \le 0.6\,\lambda\, m\sigma\,(S+x), \qquad m_{\min} = \frac{L/S}{0.6\,\lambda\,\sigma}.$$

The engine applies the first form to every trade, lowering the risk when the balance has fallen; the search starts its stops at \(m_{\min}\). Where a firm's leverage differs between evaluation and funded accounts (GFT: 1:20 then 1:10 on indices), the funded leverage sets \(m_{\min}\). FunderPro's funded accounts have a rule of their own: at most 20% of the starting balance as margin per asset class. At 1:30 on the Nasdaq and 1.5% risk that needs \(m \ge 0.913\); version 6's FunderPro setting (\(m = 0.35\)) used about 52% and broke it.

Chapter 11

Checking the engine against the formulas

The engine and the chain were written separately. The chain leaves out daily limits, minimum and profitable days, the per-day part of the concentration policy, payout dates (it pays only at the payout target, while the plan withdraws whatever profit there is on each payout date), the daily flat and weekend closes; the engine includes them. One effect exists only in the engine and only if positions are held over a weekend: a gap through the floor is absorbed by the firm, a gap through the target is kept. The plan is flat at weekends, so it does not arise; the verification stays flat at weekends too. Agreement where both apply is supporting evidence that each implements its model as described. It is not proof that either is right: two programs can agree because they share a wrong rule input or a wrong assumption. Where they differ, the difference should come from what the chain leaves out, and the third column of 11.2 measures exactly that, by running the engine with those rules switched off.

27.2 Four 100K accounts or two 200K?

Scale every dollar amount of a programme by \(\lambda\) (account, targets, limits, risk, payout target, safety margins). The engine's rules then scale exactly: every trade's win, loss and cost scale by \(\lambda\), the pass probabilities and the time are unchanged, and the funded account's trading cash scales by \(\lambda\). The fee need not scale, and the refund follows the fee actually paid. Writing \(G(S)\) for the funded cash excluding the refund, \(R(S)\) for the refund and \(q_R\) for the chance of reaching the payout that carries it (given a pass):

Scaling an account (rules that scale) $$\mathbb{E}[V(\lambda S)] = P\big[\lambda\, G(S) + q_R\, R(\lambda S)\big] - F(\lambda S), \qquad \mathbb{E}[T](\lambda S) = \mathbb{E}[T](S).$$

With \(R = F\), one account of size \(\lambda S\) is worth more than \(\lambda\) accounts of size \(S\) by \(\big[\lambda F(S) - F(\lambda S)\big](1 - P\,q_R)\) per attempt: the fee saving, less the part of it that would have come back as a refund. (Version 6's formula scaled the refund with the account; it does not.) The formula does not apply where a rule is a fixed dollar amount: GFT's $3,000 day cap and $10,000 payout cap, Maven's $10,000 rolling cap, The5ers' $150 minimum payout, and whole futures contracts. Chapter 28

Running every account at once

28.1 What adds up and what does not

By linearity (2.1), the expected cash of a set of slots over a horizon is the sum of their expected cash over that horizon, under the policy actually run, however correlated the accounts are. Correlation changes the spread: in a bad week every Nasdaq account loses together, fees fall due together, and a finite budget can run short together.

28.2 One direction per market

No two of your accounts ever hold opposite positions on one market at the same time. When a new trade opens and another of your accounts already holds that market, the new trade takes the same direction; when none does, the plan's rule decides (the sign of the last five hourly closes). The rule has no edge (Chapter 11); its job is to keep every account on one side.

28.3 The lockstep simulation

The portfolio results come from an engine that runs every account of one person on shared price paths and one calendar (lockstep.py), processing trades in the order of their entry time so the shared-direction rule is exact, applying every firm's rules (with the plan's policies) to its own accounts, and writing every fee, credit, phase result and payout to a dated ledger. The settings were frozen before any portfolio run, and each simulated life runs on its own new paths (seeds 100,000 and up), used by nothing else; version 6 re-used the paths that had chosen its settings. Lives are therefore independent and their average is an unbiased estimate with an ordinary interval. In 8,400 simulated years, no two accounts held opposite positions.

Chapter 29

If payouts can be refused

Every firm keeps the right to refuse a payout it judges to break its rules. Nobody publishes how often it happens. This chapter turns that uncertainty into scenarios; it does not cover payouts lost because a firm judges the strategy itself to break a behavioural rule (Chapter 31.2), which would not be random.

29.1 A model

Suppose each payout request at a firm is refused with probability \(\rho\), independently, and a refusal ends your use of that firm. The number of paid requests before the first refusal is geometric, with mean \((1-\rho)/\rho\). If the firm's accounts make requests at rate \(r\) a month, each of gross size \(a\), the expected cash actually paid over \(H\) months is

Paid cash under refusals $$a\,r\,(1-\rho)\,\frac{1 - e^{-\rho r H}}{\rho r} \;=\; a\,r\,H \times (1-\rho)\,\frac{1 - e^{-\rho r H}}{\rho r H},$$

the all-paid cash \(arH\) times the average survival fraction and the factor \((1-\rho)\): the refused request itself is not paid (version 6's formula left that factor out; its simulation did not). Fees paid while the firm is still used count against this. In the recommended plan the FTMO accounts together make about 1.09 requests a month: at \(\rho = 2\%\), FTMO is expected to last \(1/(0.02 \times 1.09) = 46\) months.

30.2 Placing a trade, step by step

  1. Is a trade allowed now? Weekdays, inside the market's entry hours and not from 19:00 UTC onward; not before 00:05 UTC (after the midnight resets); no restricted news window for this firm, stage and market coming up before the trade could reasonably end; at least 10 minutes since this account's last losing trade in the same direction.
  2. Direction. If any of your accounts holds this market, the same direction. Otherwise the plan's rule: long if the last hourly close is above the close five hours earlier, short if below. The rule has no edge (Chapter 11); its job is that no two of your accounts are ever opposite.
  3. Room. Floor room \(= (\text{balance} - \text{floor} - \$100 \text{ per } \$100\text{K})/(1+\rho)\); day room \(= (\text{daily limit} - \text{today's loss} - \$200 \text{ per } \$100\text{K})/(1+\rho)\); margin room \(= 0.6\,\lambda\, m\sigma \times \text{balance}\). If the floor room is under a tenth of the normal risk, stop using this account and buy its replacement. If only the day room is, wait until tomorrow.
  4. Risk. \(l\) = the smallest of the plan's risk and the three rooms. Stop: \(m\sigma\times\) price; size \(= l /\) (stop in points \(\times\) dollars per point per lot), rounded down (whole contracts on futures).
  5. Take-profit. \(w\) = the smallest of \(k\,l\), the distance to the current target, 40% of the current target less today's profit, and any firm cap (FXIFY 25% of the payout per day, Maven 20% per trade, GFT's $3,000 a day less today's profit). At Maven, if \(w < 1.5\,l\), lower \(l\) to \(w/1.5\). Distance = stop distance \(\times (w + c)/l\).
  6. Place one bracket order; never move it. At 20:00 UTC close everything still open.
  7. Log it: time, account, market, direction, size, stop, target, fill, and the closing reason. The log is the evidence for any review.

30.3 Phases, payouts and refills

  • When a phase target (plus $20 per $100K) is reached: stop. Where the firm counts trading days, place one minimal trade, held at least 2 minutes, on each remaining weekday; where it counts profitable days, the daily target of 6.4 has met it.
  • When a funded account reaches its payout target: stop, request the payout on the first allowed day (at Maven, only when the 30-day window has room for all of it, and only with the largest winning trade at most 20% of the payout), withdraw everything above the start, and start the next cycle.
  • When a payout date arrives before the target is reached, request whatever profit the account holds, if the firm's minimum and its consistency rules allow it (FXIFY: no day above 25% of it; Hola Prime: 40%; Maven: no trade above 20%), and start the next cycle; if a rule does not allow it, keep trading toward a target large enough for the rule to hold.
  • When an account fails or is closed under the floor rule: buy the next evaluation of the same programme (at FundedNext, after a pause if its gambling indicators are a concern: 28.7). Never trade an account to lose it, never let one lapse deliberately while it can still be traded under the rules.
  • Credits (The5ers) are spent on the next evaluation at that firm.

31.2 Behavioural rules the model cannot state

These rules are applied at the firm's discretion. The figures in this document assume the plan's trading is not judged to break them; if it is, the affected payouts are lost, which is not the same as the random refusals of Chapter 29:

  • FTMO: gambling, "all-in" trading, account rolling; no gap trading within 2 hours of a close.
  • Fintokei: reaching a target in one or a few identical trades; token trades. The plan's three-winning-day minimum and minimal filler trades held 2 minutes are its answer.
  • The5ers: one-trade target attainment (permanent ban). No trade makes more than 40% of a target.
  • FundedNext: one-sided betting (remedy: a 1% risk limit), account rolling, several breached accounts in a short span (remedies: 40% consistency on on-demand rewards or a lower split). Section 28.7 measures the remedies.
  • Hola Prime: profitability "materially dependent on concentrated outcomes". Alpha: "all or nothing", risk or loss of 2% or more, trades under 2 minutes on average. FXIFY: gambling. Blue Guardian: margin above 80%. Maven: large bets at 1:1.25 or worse.

31.3 Payouts refused, accounts closed

Chapter 29: $47,758 a month if payouts are refused at 2/5/15% per request by tier, $32,157 at 5/10/30%.

31.4 Spreads, gaps and news

The engine's cost is an average. Spreads widen at the daily break (now outside the plan's hours), at the open and around news. The news proxy of Section 28.7 bounds the effect of staying flat around releases.

31.5 Rules change

Every rule in Part II was read on 4 October 2026, from the pages listed in Appendix E. Leverage, consistency rules, payout schedules, prices and per-person limits change often. Recheck before buying and rerun the programme's optimisation (optimize_v7.py) when a rule that enters the model changes.

31.6 The model itself

  • Zero skill. If entries are systematically late or fills worse, the edge is negative; the cost stress is the guide.
  • Markets move together. The baseline gives gold, euro and yen independent paths; Section 28.7 measures a correlation of 0.3. Real correlations change and rise in crises.
  • Taxes and payment costs are not modelled.

31.7 What is not allowed, and is not in the plan

No hedging across accounts or firms; no accounts in anyone else's name; no copy-trading services or other people's signals; no trading of restricted news windows; no tick scalping, latency or gap arbitrage; no grid or martingale sizing; no deliberately failed accounts. Do not share or sell the plan: several firms treat traders who run the same marketed strategy as a group.

Appendix C

Glossary

AbandonmentClosing an account (no more trades, replacement bought) when its room above the floor is too small for a real trade (5.4).
Allowance \(D\)The funded account's maximum loss: the floor sits \(D\) below the starting balance.
AttemptOne evaluation bought, from purchase until a phase fails or the funded account that followed it ends.
Bold playStaking enough on each bet to reach the goal in one win; not used by the plan (5.6).
BracketA position with its stop-loss and take-profit attached at entry and never moved.
Concentration policyNo trade and no day above 40% of the current target (6.7).
Daily flatNo entry from 19:00 UTC, everything closed at 20:00 UTC (6.5).
EngineThe rule-by-rule simulation of an account on hourly price paths (acct_mc.py, pathfirm.py, lockstep.py).
Exact chainThe trade-level Markov chain of 5.5, solved by iteration (analytic_v7.py).
Payout target \(X\)The profit at which a funded account stops and requests a payout.
Renewal–rewardThe long-run value per unit time of a repeated process: \(\mathbb{E}[V]/\mathbb{E}[T]\).
SlotOne account position at one firm, refilled with a new evaluation whenever an attempt ends.
TierA firm's reliability class (A, B, D) from public evidence, used for the refusal scenarios.
Winner's curseThe upward bias of the best of many noisy estimates; removed by reporting every figure from paths not used to choose it.
