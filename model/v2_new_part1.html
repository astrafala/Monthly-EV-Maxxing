Chapter 8

Time: how long an attempt takes

Expected value per attempt is only half the story. If one plan earns $1,400 per attempt and needs 86 weeks, and another earns $1,400 and needs 3 weeks, the second is worth almost thirty times more per month. This chapter derives how long each phase takes and which choices make it faster.

8.1 How many trades a phase needs

Take fair trades that risk L and aim for k × L (reward-to-risk 1 : k). Each trade adds a random amount with mean zero and variance k × L2. A second fair-game identity (Wald's) says the expected squared final result equals the expected number of trades times the variance per trade. The final result is +A with probability B ÷ (A + B) and −B otherwise, so its expected square is A × B.

Expected number of trades in a phaseN = A × B ÷ (k × L2)

FTMO Phase 1 (A = B = $10,000) with 1% risk and 1 : 1 needs about 100 trades. With 1.5% risk and 1 : 3 it needs about 10,000 × 10,000 ÷ (3 × 1,5002) ≈ 15 trades.

8.2 How long one trade takes

A bracket closes when price moves a distance s against you or k × s in your favour. If the price moves with volatility σ per hour (its typical one-hour move), the same identity applied to price gives:

Expected duration of one bracketTtrade ≈ k × s2 ÷ σ2    hours

A stop of one hourly move (s = σ) at 1 : 1 takes about one active hour. A stop of three hourly moves takes about nine.

8.3 Time and cost of a whole phase

Multiply the two. The reward-to-risk ratio k cancels:

Phase time and phase costTime ≈ A × B × s2 ÷ (L2 × σ2)      Cost ≈ A × B × c ÷ (k × L × s)

Here c is the round-trip cost as a fraction of price. These two lines contain every lever:

8.4 The speed–cost trade-off

A tighter stop finishes the phase sooner but costs more per dollar risked. If the expected value per attempt falls roughly in a straight line with cost, EV ≈ EV0 − β / s, and time grows like s2, then EV per unit time is (EV0 − β/s) ÷ s2. Setting its derivative to zero gives s = 1.5 β ÷ EV0, where EV is one third of EV0.

The surprising rule

When time is what matters, the best stop is tight enough that trading costs eat about two thirds of the no-cost expected value. Fixed waiting times (minimum days, reviews, the 14-day payout calendar) push the optimum back towards wider stops, because once those waits dominate, more speed buys little. Chapter 10 finds the real optimum by simulation.

8.5 Fixed waits you cannot trade away

  • Minimum trading days: 4 per phase at FTMO, 3 at FundingPips and The5ers, 5 at FundedNext.
  • Reviews: about 1 day between phases and 1–3 days for identity checks and the contract before funding.
  • The payout calendar: FTMO's first payout comes 14 days after your first funded trade, then every 14 days. FundingPips offers weekly payouts at a 60% split. FundedNext's first cycle is 21 days. Crypto firms such as Breakout and HyroTrader pay on demand.

These waits set a floor. Even the fastest pass cannot produce an FTMO payout much sooner than about 25 days after purchase: 4 + 4 minimum trading days, about 4 days of reviews, then 14 days to the first payout date.

Chapter 9

Instruments, leverage and platforms

9.1 The one number that ranks instruments

From Section 8.3, for a fixed time budget, the cost you pay is proportional to c ÷ σ: the trading cost compared with how far the instrument moves. Over a full trading week this becomes:

Instrument efficiencyEfficiency = typical weekly move ÷ round-trip trading cost     (higher = faster for the same cost)

Weekly movement already includes trading hours. An instrument that trades 24/7 gets credit for its weekend movement automatically, so "crypto trades all week" is already counted.

9.2 Crypto: trading 24/7 does not help

Bitcoin moves about six times as much as EURUSD in a week, partly because it trades on weekends. But on prop platforms it costs fifteen to twenty times as much per trade, because crypto carries percentage commissions (0.0325% per side at FTMO, 0.04% at Breakout, 0.055% taker at Bybit). Its efficiency ends up between a half and a tenth of the alternatives. Chapter 10 confirms it: the best crypto set-up found earns about a fifth of the Nasdaq plan per month.

9.3 What leverage actually does

Leverage does not make trades resolve faster. Time depends on how far price must travel (the stop distance) compared with how fast it moves. Leverage only limits position size, and so sets the tightest stop you can use for a given risk:

Tightest stop allowed by leverage λsmin = risk per trade ÷ (λ × account size)     1.5% risk at 1:50 → 0.03%; at 1:3.3 → 0.45%

For indices, metals and currencies the limit is far below any sensible stop. For crypto at FTMO (1:3.3), 0.45% is just above one hourly Bitcoin move, so leverage would block the fastest crypto stops. Costs make those stops unattractive anyway.

9.4 Overnight financing

Positions held through the daily rollover pay (or occasionally earn) financing on their full size. With tight stops the position is large, so even one night can cost several percent of the risk on index and gold CFDs. Fast plans close most trades within hours, which keeps this small. The simulations charge it on every rollover crossed.

9.5 Futures

Futures are the cheapest instrument of all, but futures prop firms use trailing drawdowns, small accounts ($2,000 allowance on 50K), payout ladders and consistency rules (Chapter 11). Their value per account is small, though attempts are quick and some firms allow many accounts at once.

Chapter 10

Maximising expected value per month

10.1 Method

For each instrument we measured, on real hourly data, how long random-direction brackets of each size actually take (including waiting for trading hours and weekends) and how often they cross the rollover. Those measured durations then drive a full simulation of each firm's contract with calendar time:

  • Daily loss limits, minimum days and reviews.
  • Payout calendars and refunds.
  • A daily guard that stops trading before the daily limit can be breached.

Every set-up was simulated 1,200–4,000 times. The trades are assumed to have no edge: the small win-rate deviations seen in the data were treated as noise. The result is expected value per month per account slot = expected value per attempt ÷ average length of an attempt.
