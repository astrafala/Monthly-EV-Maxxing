What the number means, and what it does not

  • It is an expected value: the average over many possible years. A single year's cash is spread widely (the percentiles above) because funded-account payouts come in large, rare pieces.
  • It assumes zero skill: every trade is a fair bet before costs. The value comes from the contract (a capped loss, a share of the gains), not from predicting prices. Part I proves why that is positive and how large.
  • It assumes the firms pay as their rules say. The two scenarios above price the risk that they do not. That risk is real, and not measurable from public data.
  • It assumes the costs measured in Chapter 3. Chapter 31 reruns every programme at 1.5 and 2 times those costs.
  • It is per person: every firm limits what one person may hold, and Chapter 27 fills each limit. Accounts in anyone else's name are not allowed and are not in the plan.

How it was found

  1. Part I derives every formula from first principles: the bracket trade, the pass probability (exactly, for any reward-to-risk), the funded account's payout (the withdrawal identity), time and value per month.

  3. Every reported figure was re-measured on price paths not used to choose it, and on the real 2024–26 history, and checked against exact equations written separately (Chapter 11).
  4. The accounts were then run together, one person at a time, on one shared price path and calendar, 2,800 simulated years in all (Chapter 28).

Part I

The mathematics, from zero

Every result in this document follows from a handful of facts about fair games. This part derives each of them from the beginning, with the numbers of the real plan worked through at every step. Nothing is assumed that is not proved or measured.

Chapter 1

What is being bought

A proprietary trading firm ("prop firm") sells an evaluation. You pay a fee, you trade a simulated account under written rules, and if you meet the profit targets without breaking a loss limit, the firm gives you a funded account. On the funded account you keep a share of every profit you withdraw, and the firm absorbs every loss. You never owe the firm money beyond the fee.

That last sentence is the whole opportunity. Your downside is capped at the fee, and your upside is a share of profits that, as Chapter 7 proves, have a positive expected size even when every trade is a coin flip. The contract is an option: you pay a premium (the fee) for the right to keep part of the gains of a game whose losses fall on someone else.

1.2 The value of one attempt

Call the cash an attempt brings you \(V\). It is the fee (always paid), plus, if both phases are passed, whatever the funded account pays out (the refund included):

Value of one attempt $$V = -F + \mathbf{1}\{\text{pass}\}\cdot C, \qquad \mathbb{E}[V] = -F + \Pr(\text{pass})\cdot \mathbb{E}[C \mid \text{pass}]$$

Here \(\mathbf{1}\{\text{pass}\}\) is 1 if both phases are passed and 0 otherwise, and \(C\) is the total cash from the funded account. The second form uses the fact that the expectation of a product with an indicator is the probability times the conditional expectation. The rest of Part I computes the two unknowns, \(\Pr(\text{pass})\) and \(\mathbb{E}[C\mid\text{pass}]\), and then the time an attempt takes.

1.3 The whole argument in five lines

Before the details, the result in its simplest form, with no trading costs and no time. Chapter 5 proves that a fair game reaches a target \(A\) above its start before a floor \(B\) below with probability \(B/(A+B)\). Chapter 7 proves that a funded account whose profits are withdrawn every cycle pays out, on average, exactly its allowance \(D\) before it is lost. Therefore:

FTMO 2-Step, zero cost, zero skill $$\Pr(\text{pass}) = \frac{B}{A_1+B}\cdot\frac{B}{A_2+B} = \frac{10{,}000}{20{,}000}\cdot\frac{10{,}000}{15{,}000} = \frac{1}{3}$$ $$\mathbb{E}[V] = -632 + \tfrac13\left(0.8\times 10{,}000 + 632\right) = -632 + 2{,}877 = +\$2{,}245$$

A coin-flipper who buys this contract makes, on average, $2,245 per attempt. Everything else in this document refines this number: trading costs lower it, daily limits and rules change the time it takes, and the plan's settings are chosen to make the most of it per month and per person. The refinement takes the full engine of Part I; the sign of the answer is already settled here.

1.4 Why the firm still makes money

The firm's revenue is the fees of everyone, and most traders are not zero-skill coin-flippers with a plan built around the contract: they over-trade, break rules, abandon accounts and stop after one failure. The firm also pays out simulated profits only, so it carries no market risk of its own unless it copies trades. A plan that treats the contract as a mathematical object, follows every rule, and repeats it many times is the case the firm's pricing does not cover. That is also why every firm keeps the right to refuse payouts it judges to be "gambling" (Chapter 29) and why this plan follows every written rule.

Chapter 2

The probability toolkit

Seven ideas carry the whole analysis. Each is stated, explained and, where short, proved.

2.1 Expected value and linearity

A random quantity \(X\) that takes values \(x_1, x_2, \dots\) with probabilities \(p_1, p_2, \dots\) has expected value

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_i p_i\, x_i .$$

The single most useful fact is linearity: for any random quantities, dependent or not,

$$\mathbb{E}[X+Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y], \qquad \mathbb{E}[cX] = c\,\mathbb{E}[X].$$

Linearity is why the value of many accounts is the sum of their values, however strongly they move together (Chapter 28). Correlation changes the spread of outcomes, never the average.

2.2 Variance

The variance measures spread around the mean: \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2\). Its square root is the standard deviation. For independent \(X, Y\) variances add, \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y) = \operatorname{Var}X + \operatorname{Var}Y\); for dependent ones a covariance term \(2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\) is added. Variance enters in two places: the time a fair game takes to reach a target (Chapter 8), and the confidence intervals of every simulated number (Chapter 9).

2.3 Fair games and martingales

A sequence of account balances \(X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots\) (one per trade, or per instant) is a martingale if, given everything known at step \(n\), the expected next value is the current one:

$$\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid X_0,\dots,X_n] = X_n .$$

"Zero skill" means exactly this for the account balance before costs: no rule for choosing direction, size or timing can make the expected next balance higher than the current one, because the price itself is a martingale and the balance is a sum of positions times price changes. With costs, the expected next balance is lower by the cost: the balance is a supermartingale.

2.4 Stopping times and the optional stopping theorem

A stopping time \(\tau\) is a rule for when to stop that uses only information available at the time: "stop when the balance first reaches the target or the floor" is one; "stop the day before the price falls" is not. The optional stopping theorem says that for a martingale and a stopping time that is almost surely finite, with the martingale bounded up to \(\tau\) (true here: the balance lives between the floor and the target),

$$\mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = X_0 .$$

No stopping rule turns a fair game into a profitable one. This single theorem gives the pass probability (Chapter 5), the win probability of a bracket (Chapter 4) and the funded account's value (Chapter 7).

2.5 Wald's identity

If each step adds an amount with mean \(\mu\) (the same every step) and \(\tau\) is a stopping time with finite mean, then the total gained by time \(\tau\) has mean

$$\mathbb{E}[X_\tau - X_0] = \mu\,\mathbb{E}[\tau].$$

It converts a known final distribution into an expected number of steps: it gives the expected number of trades in a phase when trades cost money (Chapter 5).

2.6 Brownian motion

The continuous-time limit of a fair random walk is Brownian motion \(W_t\): it starts at 0, its increments over disjoint intervals are independent, and \(W_{t+h} - W_t\) is normally distributed with mean 0 and variance \(h\). A process \(X_t = \mu t + \sigma W_t\) has drift \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) per unit time. Three of its martingales carry most of the analysis:

$$X_t - \mu t, \qquad (X_t-\mu t)^2 - \sigma^2 t, \qquad \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{2\mu}{\sigma^2} X_t\right).$$

The first is the definition of drift. The second follows because the variance grows linearly in time. The third is the exponential martingale: for \(\lambda = -2\mu/\sigma^2\), Itô's formula gives \(d e^{\lambda X} = e^{\lambda X}(\lambda\mu + \tfrac12\lambda^2\sigma^2)\,dt + (\dots)\,dW\), and the \(dt\) term vanishes exactly when \(\lambda = -2\mu/\sigma^2\).

2.7 The reflection principle

For a Brownian path that goes from \(a\) to \(b\) over a time interval with variance \(v\), the chance that it rose above a level \(y \ge \max(a,b)\) somewhere in between is

$$\Pr\big(\max \ge y \,\big|\, a \to b\big) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{2(y-a)(y-b)}{v}\right).$$

Reflect the path at its first touch of \(y\): every path that touches \(y\) and ends at \(b\) corresponds one-to-one to a path that ends at \(2y-b\), whose density relative to ending at \(b\) is the exponential above. This formula is how the simulation draws the highs and lows inside each hour exactly (Chapter 3), so that stops and targets are touched as often as a real continuous price touches them.

3.2 Zero drift

The model gives the price no trend at all. This is the zero-skill assumption: whatever direction rule the plan uses, it has no information about the next move. The Nasdaq has risen on average over decades, but that drift is about 0.0025% per hour, roughly a hundredth of the hourly volatility, and the plan is long and short equally often, so it neither helps nor hurts.

3.3 Synthetic paths

Results are computed on synthetic price histories built to be exactly fair:

  1. Twelve years of hourly bars per path. Each hour is split into 12 sub-steps of independent normal increments with variance \(\sigma^2/12\), less a correction \(-\sigma^2/24\) per step so that the price itself, not just its logarithm, has zero drift.
  2. Within each sub-step the highest and lowest point is drawn from the exact distribution of a Brownian bridge (2.7): with endpoints \(a,b\) and variance \(v\), the maximum is $$\max = \tfrac12\left(a+b+\sqrt{(b-a)^2 - 2v\ln U}\right),\qquad U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1),$$ which is the inverse of the reflection-principle distribution. The minimum is the mirror image.
  3. Markets that close at weekends are closed in the path from Friday to Sunday, but the price keeps moving underneath, so Monday can open with a gap.
  4. Each path is fixed by its random seed, so any path can be rebuilt exactly. The optimiser searches on paths 21–24, confirms on 25–28 and reports final figures on 29–32 (33–36 for the one setting re-chosen for robustness), so no reported number is measured on the paths that chose the settings (Chapter 9). Gold, USDJPY and EURUSD accounts use their own independent paths (seed + 50, + 60, + 70), because those markets do not move with the Nasdaq.

3.4 Real paths

Every final figure is also run on the real 2024–26 hourly history of its market. One two-year history carries its own trends and calm or wild months, so real-path figures scatter around the synthetic ones by a few hundred dollars a month. They are a check, not the estimate.

Chapter 4

The bracket trade

Every trade in the plan is a bracket: one position with a stop-loss and a take-profit attached the moment it opens, never moved. This chapter derives everything about one such trade.

4.1 Set-up

Open a long position at price \(e\) with notional size \(N\) dollars (a short position is the mirror image). Place the stop a fraction \(s\) below the entry and the target a fraction \(u\) above:

$$\text{stop} = e(1-s), \qquad \text{target} = e(1+u).$$

The plan fixes three things and derives the rest:

  • the risk \(l\): what a stopped-out trade loses before costs, 1.5% of the account;
  • the stop distance \(s = m\sigma\): \(m\) hourly standard deviations of the market;
  • the reward-to-risk ratio \(k\): a winning trade nets \(w = k\,l\) after costs.

The notional follows from the risk and the stop, and the target from the requirement that a win nets exactly \(w\) after the round-trip cost \(c\):

Position and target $$N = \frac{l}{s}, \qquad N u - c = w \;\Rightarrow\; u = \frac{s\,(w+c)}{l}.$$

So a win pays \(+w\) and a loss costs \(-(l+c)\): the cost is paid on every trade, and the target is moved out by exactly the cost so that a win is worth what the plan intends. (Chapter 17 of version 4 found that a take-profit set without the cost turns the last "win" before a target into a small loss; this is that fix, in formula form.)

4.2 The win probability

The price is a martingale and the trade ends the first time it touches the stop or the target, a stopping time. By optional stopping (2.4), the expected exit price equals the entry price:

$$p\cdot e(1+u) + (1-p)\cdot e(1-s) = e \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; p\,u = (1-p)\,s \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; p = \frac{s}{s+u}.$$

Substituting \(u\):

Win probability of a zero-skill bracket $$p = \frac{s}{s + s(w+c)/l} = \frac{l}{l + w + c} = \frac{1}{1 + k + c/l}.$$

With no cost, a 1 : 5 bracket wins one time in six. The cost makes the target slightly farther and the win slightly rarer.

4.3 The mean and the variance of one trade

The trade pays \(+w\) with probability \(p\) and \(-(l+c)\) otherwise. Its mean is

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{pnl}] = p\,w - (1-p)(l+c) = \frac{l\,w - (w+c)(l+c)}{l+w+c} = \frac{-c\,(l+w+c)}{l+w+c} = -c .$$

For the variance, look at the result before cost, \(Y = \text{pnl} + c\). It takes the values \(w+c\) (probability \(p\)) and \(-l\), and its mean is \(p(w+c) - (1-p)l = \frac{l(w+c) - (w+c)l}{l+w+c} = 0\). So

$$\operatorname{Var}(\text{pnl}) = \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}[Y^2] = p\,(w+c)^2 + (1-p)\,l^2 = \frac{l\,(w+c)^2 + (w+c)\,l^2}{l+w+c} = l\,(w+c).$$
The trade's mean is minus its cost, exactly; its variance is k times the risk squared $$\mathbb{E}[\text{pnl}] = -c, \qquad v \equiv \operatorname{Var}(\text{pnl}) = l\,(w+c) = l^2\left(k + \tfrac{c}{l}\right).$$

The variance of one trade is \(k\) times the risk squared, plus a small cost term. Larger \(k\) means fewer, bigger moves of the balance; Chapter 8 shows this leaves the time to a target unchanged and lowers the total cost.

4.5 The plan's trade on the Nasdaq, worked through

The plan's FTMO setting is \(l = \$1{,}500\) (1.5% of $100,000), a stop of \(m = 0.5\) hourly standard deviations, and \(k = 20\). Take the very first trade of phase 1, when the account is at its start and the target is \(A = \$10{,}000\) away. Since \(k\,l = \$30{,}000\) is more than the distance to the target, the win is cut to \(w = \$10{,}000\) (5.5). With the Nasdaq at 31,070:

Stop distance: \(s = m\sigma = 0.5 \times 0.2737\% = 0.1368\%\) of the price, i.e. \(42.5\) index points.

Notional: \(N = l/s = 1{,}500 / 0.001368 = \$1{,}096{,}137\), i.e. \(\$35.28\) per index point (\(= l/\text{stop points}\)). If one lot is worth $1 per point (read the contract specification; some platforms use $10 or $20), that is \(35.28\) lots, rounded down to the lot step.

Cost: \(c = \kappa N = 0.0064\% \times \$1{,}096{,}137 = \$70.15\), so \(c/l = 4.68\%\).

Target: \(u = s(w+c)/l = 0.1368\% \times 10{,}070.15/1{,}500 = 0.9187\%\), i.e. \(285.4\) points above the entry (for a long).

Win probability: \(p = l/(l+w+c) = 1{,}500/(1{,}500 + 10{,}000 + 70.15) = 0.1296\).

Mean: \(-c = -\$70.15\). Check: \(p\,w - (1-p)(l+c) = 0.12964\times10{,}000 - 0.87036\times1{,}570.15 = -70.15\).

Variance: \(v = l(w+c) = 1{,}500\times10{,}070.15 = 15{,}105{,}229\), standard deviation \(\$3{,}887\).

The engine charges every one of those nights.

4.6 Four ways a simulation can flatter a trade

Each of these was found and fixed in earlier versions; each makes a zero-skill bracket look profitable when it is not.

  1. Skipping the entry bar. If stops and targets are checked only from the next hour, a move that would have stopped the trade inside the entry hour is ignored. Measured effect on a 1 : 3 bracket: +0.14 units of risk per trade, all of it fake.
  2. Checking prices too rarely. A price sampled a few times an hour misses touches in between; the stop (closer) is missed more often than the target. Effect: +0.11 R. The Brownian-bridge extremes (3.3) remove it.
  3. Ties inside one bar. When one hourly bar's range contains both the stop and the target, the bar alone does not say which came first. Version 5's engine (and the first run of this version) decided it with the overall fair chance \(l/(l+w+c)\). That chance is right for the whole trade but not for a trade given that both levels fell inside one hour, and for narrow brackets (a stop of 0.35 hourly moves with \(k = 2\) or 3) it gave the trade an edge of +0.025 ± 0.016 R at k = 2 and +0.034 ± 0.020 R at k = 3 per trade, enough to lift FundingPips' capped phase 2 from its exact zero-cost value of 66.7% to 67.8% ± 0.6%. The engine now replays the twelve five-minute sub-steps of that hour in order and takes whichever level the path reached first; only a tie inside one five-minute step falls back to the fair chance. After the fix: -0.014 ± 0.011 R at k = 2 and -0.005 ± 0.014 R at k = 3, and FundingPips' phase 2 at zero cost 66.8% ± 0.6%. Every figure in this document comes from the corrected engine. On the real history, which has only hourly bars, ties still use the fair chance.
  4. Gaps. A price that opens beyond the stop (after a weekend, say) fills at the open, not at the stop. The engine fills it at the open; the loss can then exceed \(l\), and if it breaks a daily or maximum loss limit the account fails, as it would in reality.

The engine's checks against theory are in Chapter 11: a 1 : 1 bracket wins 50.24% of the time (theory 50%), a 1 : 2 bracket 32.86% (33.3%), and the average result of a 1 : 3 bracket is +0.017 ± 0.018 R (theory 0).

Chapter 5

Reaching a target before a floor

A phase of an evaluation is passed when the balance gains \(A\) before it loses \(B\). This is the oldest problem in probability, the gambler's ruin. This chapter solves it exactly with and without costs, and counts the trades it takes.

5.1 No costs: \(P = B/(A+B)\)

Let \(X_n\) be the balance relative to the start after \(n\) trades, a martingale with \(X_0 = 0\), and \(\tau\) the first time it reaches \(+A\) or \(-B\). The plan sizes trades so the balance lands exactly on the target or the floor (5.5), so \(X_\tau\) is \(+A\) with probability \(P\) and \(-B\) otherwise. Optional stopping:

$$0 = \mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = P\cdot A - (1-P)\cdot B \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \boxed{P = \frac{B}{A+B}}.$$

The answer depends on nothing but the two distances: not on \(k\), not on the stop, not on how many trades it takes. No way of trading changes it. This is why "skill-free" pass rates are so predictable: FTMO phase 1 is a coin flip (\(10/20 = 50\%\)), phase 2 is two in three (\(10/15\)).

5.2 With costs: the exponential martingale

With a cost \(c\) per trade the balance drifts down by \(c\) per trade and has variance \(v\) per trade (4.3). Treat it as a Brownian motion with drift \(-c\) and variance \(v\) per trade. By 2.6, \(e^{\theta X}\) is a martingale for

$$\theta = \frac{2c}{v}.$$

Optional stopping on it:

$$1 = \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\theta X_\tau}\right] = P e^{\theta A} + (1-P)e^{-\theta B}.$$
Pass probability with costs $$P = \frac{1 - e^{-\theta B}}{e^{\theta A} - e^{-\theta B}} = \frac{e^{\theta B} - 1}{e^{\theta (A+B)} - 1}, \qquad \theta = \frac{2c}{v} = \frac{2\,c/l}{l\,(k + c/l)} .$$

As \(c\to 0\), \(\theta\to 0\) and \(P \to B/(A+B)\). For small \(\theta\), expanding both exponentials to second order,

$$P \approx \frac{B}{A+B}\left(1 - \frac{\theta A}{2}\right), \qquad \frac{\theta A}{2} = \frac{c\,A}{v} \approx \frac{(c/l)\,(A/l)}{k}.$$

The relative loss of pass probability is the cost per trade in units of risk, times the target in units of risk, divided by \(k\). Three levers reduce it: a cheaper market (smaller \(c/l\)), a wider stop (also smaller \(c/l\), but slower, Chapter 8), and a larger \(k\).

5.3 How many trades: Wald's identity

Each trade adds \(-c\) on average, so by Wald (2.5), \(\mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = -c\,\mathbb{E}[\tau]\), and \(\mathbb{E}[X_\tau] = PA - (1-P)B\):

Expected number of trades in a phase $$\mathbb{E}[\tau] = \frac{(1-P)\,B - P\,A}{c} \;\xrightarrow{\;c\to0\;}\; \frac{A\,B}{v} = \frac{A\,B}{l^2 k}.$$

The zero-cost limit follows from the second martingale of 2.6, \(X_n^2 - n v\): \(\mathbb{E}[X_\tau^2] = v\,\mathbb{E}[\tau]\), and \(\mathbb{E}[X_\tau^2] = PA^2 + (1-P)B^2 = AB\) when \(P = B/(A+B)\). In units of risk, FTMO phase 1 (\(A = B = 6.67\,l\)) takes \(44.4/k\) trades: about 9 at \(k=5\), 7 at \(k=6\).

5.5 Landing exactly on the target or the floor

The formulas assume the balance ends exactly at \(+A\) or \(-B\). Two sizing rules make that true in practice:

  • Take-profit capped at what is left. When the remaining distance to the target is less than \(k\,l\), the win \(w\) is set to the remaining distance (plus cost, 4.1). Profit beyond the target would be wasted: it raises no probability and was paid for with risk.
  • Risk capped near the floor. The risk is the smallest of 1.5% of the account, the room above the floor less 0.1% of the account, and the room left in today's loss limit less 0.2%. A stop-out can then never break a limit; only a price gap through the stop can (4.6).

Both rules keep every trade a fair bracket, so the martingale arguments still apply exactly; they only change the number of trades.

5.6 The exact chain, and bold play

The formulas of 5.2 treat the balance as a Brownian motion, which is accurate when each trade is small next to the distances \(A\) and \(B\). The plan's optimised settings are not like that: with \(k = 30\) a single win is worth 45% of the account, so the take-profit is almost always cut to the distance that is left (5.5), and one win ends the phase. For such settings the balance is better described exactly, as a chain that jumps from trade to trade. At a balance \(x\) above the start (\(-B < x < A\)) the next trade has

One step of the exact chain $$l(x) = \min\big(L,\; x + B - \text{buffer}\big), \qquad w(x) = \min\big(k\,l(x),\; A - x,\; \text{cap}\big), \qquad c(x) = \rho\, l(x), \qquad p(x) = \frac{l}{l + w + c},$$ $$x \;\to\; x + w \text{ with probability } p, \qquad x \;\to\; x - l - c \text{ otherwise},$$

where \(L\) is the normal risk, \(\rho = \kappa/(m\sigma)\) the cost per dollar of risk, and the cap is any limit a rule puts on one win (a best-day share, a daily profit cap, FundingPips' profit concentration). The chance \(V(x)\) of reaching \(A\) before \(-B\), the expected number of trades \(N(x)\) and the expected total cost \(K(x)\) solve

$$V(x) = p\,V(x+w) + (1-p)\,V(x-l-c), \qquad N(x) = 1 + p\,N(x+w) + (1-p)\,N(x-l-c), \qquad K(x) = c + p\,K(x+w) + (1-p)\,K(x-l-c),$$

with \(V = 1\) at the target, \(V = 0\) at the floor and \(N = K = 0\) at both. These are solved by iteration on a grid of 6,000 points (analytic_v6.py). Two exact facts check the solution. First, optional stopping on the balance (whose mean falls by exactly the cost of each trade) gives

Pass probability, exact, any k $$P\cdot A - (1-P)\cdot B = -K(0) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P = \frac{B - \mathbb{E}[\text{total cost}]}{A + B}.$$

Every dollar of expected cost lowers the pass probability by exactly \(1/(A+B)\). Second, when \(k\,L \ge A + B\) every win ends the phase, and the chain becomes a simple sequence: the \(j\)-th trade starts at \(x_j = -j\,(L + c)\) and wins the whole distance \(A - x_j\) with chance \(p_j = L/(L + A - x_j + c)\), so

Bold play, written out $$P = 1 - \prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(1 - p_j\right) = p_0 + (1-p_0)\,p_1 + (1-p_0)(1-p_1)\,p_2 + \cdots$$

The last factor uses the smaller risk that fits above the floor. This is bold play, studied by Dubins and Savage (1965): in a game that is fair or slightly unfavourable, the strategy that maximises the chance of reaching a goal is to stake, each time, enough to reach the goal in one win (or everything, if that is less). The reason is the cost: each trade loses \(c\) on average, and the expected total cost \(K(0)\) is what lowers \(P\). Bold play reaches the goal or the floor in the fewest trades, so it pays the least cost. Chapter 9 finds that the optimiser, left free, moves every programme toward this corner.

Chapter 6

The evaluation in full

6.1 Two phases

A 2-step evaluation starts phase 2 on a fresh account once phase 1 is passed. The phases share nothing but the trader, so with zero skill they are independent and

$$\Pr(\text{pass}) = P_1 \cdot P_2 .$$

The order of the targets does not matter for this product when costs are zero (\(B/(A_1+B)\cdot B/(A_2+B)\) is symmetric), but it does for time and cost: the larger target first means fewer second phases are ever started, which saves the second phase's trades on the attempts that would fail anyway.

6.2 The daily loss limit

Most firms add a second floor that moves: the balance (or equity) may not fall more than \(d\) below its level at the start of the day. Breaking it fails the account at once. The plan never risks breaking it by its own stop: each trade's risk is at most today's remaining room less 0.2% of the account, and when less than a tenth of the normal risk remains, it stops for the day. With a 1.5% risk and a 4–5% limit, that takes two or three losses in one day, which happens on a minority of days (counted in each programme's chapter).

Because the plan reacts to the daily limit only by trading smaller or stopping, every trade is still a fair bracket and the pass probability is unchanged except through costs: smaller trades cost the same fraction of their size but there are more of them. The engine models the limit exactly, including the rare gap that breaks it.

6.3 Minimum trading days

Some firms require a minimum number of days with at least one trade in each phase (FTMO 4, FundedNext 5, BrightFunded 5, FXIFY Classic 4, Alpha 3). If the target is reached earlier, the plan places one minimal trade (the smallest lot) on each remaining required day. This is allowed at every firm that counts trading days, adds a negligible cost and only adds time. It is never done where the rule counts profitable days (6.4).

6.4 Profitable-day rules

Some firms require days with at least +0.5% profit (The5ers, Fintokei, Maven, Blue Guardian, FundingPips' 95% option), and forbid manufacturing them. The plan uses a daily profit target: while profitable days are missing, each day it trades normally until the day's profit reaches

$$g_{\text{today}} = \max\!\left(0.55\%\,S,\; \frac{\text{distance left to the target}}{\text{profitable days still needed}}\right)$$

and then stops for the day. Every trade is still a full-size fair bracket (its take-profit is capped at what is left of today's target), so nothing is manufactured. Stopping at a daily target is another stopping time, so in a fair game it leaves the probability of the final outcome unchanged; what changes is the time, because a phase now takes at least as many days as profitable days required. If the target is reached with profitable days still missing, trading continues under the daily target until they are complete, which exposes the balance a little longer and lowers the pass probability slightly (The5ers phase 1: 49.8% ± 0.7% with costs removed against 50%).

6.5 Weekends, nights and news

The plan holds no position over a weekend at any firm (no new trade after 19:00 UTC on Friday; everything closed by 20:30 UTC), places no new trade from 15 minutes before to 5 minutes after a high-impact US release, and on funded accounts is flat 10 minutes before such releases. Positions may run through the night on weekdays where the firm allows it (all CFD firms here); the futures layer is flat by 20:00 UTC every day. These rules cost a little time (forced closes end some trades early at a price between stop and target, which is still a fair outcome) and remove the largest gap risk.

6.6 Review time

Firms take one to three working days to check a passed phase and issue the next account. The engine adds one day after phase 1 and the firm's stated time after phase 2.

Chapter 7

What a funded account pays

On the funded account the plan trades each payout cycle to a profit target \(X\) above the starting balance, stops, requests the payout on the first allowed day, and withdraws every dollar above the start. The balance is back at the start, the floor (static) is still \(D\) below it, and the next cycle begins. The account ends the first time the balance touches the floor. This chapter computes what that pays.

7.1 The withdrawal identity

Let \(Y_t\) be the balance above the start plus everything withdrawn so far (before the split). A withdrawal moves money from the first term to the second and leaves \(Y\) unchanged; a trade changes \(Y\) by its result. So \(Y\) behaves exactly like the trading result itself: a fair game before costs. The account ends when the balance is at \(-D\), at which time \(Y_\tau = -D + W\), where \(W\) is the total withdrawn. Optional stopping on \(Y\), allowing for the cost \(c\) of each of the \(N_f\) funded trades (Wald):

$$\mathbb{E}[Y_\tau] = -c\,\mathbb{E}[N_f] \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathbb{E}[W] = D - c\,\mathbb{E}[N_f].$$
The withdrawal identity $$\mathbb{E}[\text{cash from a funded account}] = \alpha\left(D - c\,\mathbb{E}[N_f]\right) + R\cdot\Pr(\text{refund reached}).$$

Before costs, a funded account pays out on average its whole allowance times the split, whatever the cycle target, the stop, \(k\), the payout calendar or the trading rule. A $100,000 account with a $10,000 static allowance and an 80% split is worth $8,000 plus the refund term, less the cost of the trades it takes to get there. The identity needs only that profits are eventually withdrawn and that the account can end only at its floor; it holds with best-day rules, payout caps and minimum days, which change time, never the average.

7.2 The same result, cycle by cycle

The identity can also be built from the parts, which gives the time and the number of payouts. Each cycle is a fresh gambler's ruin from the start: reach \(+X\) before \(-D\). Its success chance is \(q\) from 5.2 with \(A = X\), \(B = D\). Cycles succeed independently until the first failure, so the number of paid cycles \(K\) satisfies \(\Pr(K \ge j) = q^j\):

$$\mathbb{E}[K] = \sum_{j\ge1} q^j = \frac{q}{1-q}.$$

With no cost \(q = D/(X+D)\), so \(\mathbb{E}[K] = D/X\) and the expected total withdrawn is \(X\cdot D/X = D\): the identity again. With a first cycle \(X_1\) different from later ones \(X\) (first-payout rules) and success chances \(q_1, q\):

Funded cash, cycle form $$\mathbb{E}[C] = \alpha\left(X_1 q_1 + X\,\frac{q_1\,q}{1-q}\right) + R\cdot q_1 q^{\,r-1},$$

where the refund comes with the \(r\)-th payout (\(r=1\) at most firms; FundingPips Standard and Blue Guardian 4, Maven 3). Where a refund is paid in \(r\) equal parts with the first \(r\) payouts (Hola Prime), the last term is \(\frac{R}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r} q_1 q^{\,j-1}\).

7.3 What the cycle target changes

Before costs, nothing about the average. With costs, the expected number of funded trades is, by the zero-cost formula of 5.3 summed over the expected \(1/(1-q) = (X+D)/X\) cycles,

$$\mathbb{E}[N_f] \approx \frac{X\,D}{v}\cdot\frac{X+D}{X} = \frac{D\,(X+D)}{v} = \frac{D\,(X+D)}{l^2 k}.$$

A larger cycle target means more trades over the account's life, more cost, and more time between payouts; a smaller one means more payout requests, each followed by waiting for the payout date. The best \(X\) balances the two, and depends on the firm's payout calendar (Chapter 9 finds it by search). It also changes how many payout requests a firm sees, which matters if payouts can be refused (Chapter 29).

7.4 Rules that change only time

  • Best-day (consistency) rules. If no day may exceed a share \(\beta\) of a payout's profit, the plan caps each day's take-profit at \(\beta X\) less what the day has already made, so a cycle needs at least \(1/\beta\) profitable days. FXIFY Classic (\(\beta = 25\%\)) needs four, Hola Prime (40%) three.
  • Profitable days per cycle (FundingPips 95%, Hola Prime): the daily profit target of 6.4, applied per cycle.
  • Payout calendars. The first payout is allowed 14 to 30 days after the account opens, then every 10 to 30 days. A cycle that reaches its target early waits, flat, for the date.
  • Payout caps (GFT: the first two payouts at most 6% of the account): the cycle target is set to the cap.
  • Daily profit caps (GFT funded $3,000 a day): the take-profit is capped at what is left of the day's cap.

Chapter 8

Time, and value per month

8.1 How long one trade lasts

For a driftless price with variance \(\sigma^2\) per hour, the second martingale of 2.6 is \(Z_t^2 - \sigma^2 t\), where \(Z\) is the log-price move since entry. Stopping when \(Z\) first touches \(+d_t\) (target) or \(-d_s\) (stop), with \(\Pr(\text{target}) = d_s/(d_s+d_t)\):

$$\sigma^2\,\mathbb{E}[\tau] = \mathbb{E}[Z_\tau^2] = \frac{d_s}{d_s+d_t}\,d_t^2 + \frac{d_t}{d_s+d_t}\,d_s^2 = d_s\,d_t .$$
Expected duration of a bracket (hours of open market) $$\mathbb{E}[\tau] = \frac{d_s\,d_t}{\sigma^2} = \frac{(m\sigma)\cdot m\sigma(k + c/l)}{\sigma^2} = m^2\,(k + c/l).$$

A 1 : 6 bracket with its stop at half an hourly move lasts on average \(0.25\times 6.03 \approx 1.5\) hours of open market; at 0.75 hourly moves, 3.4 hours. Most trades are short (the stop is close) and a few are long (the wins).

8.2 How long a phase lasts

Multiply the number of trades (5.3) by the duration of each (8.1). In the zero-cost limit:

$$\mathbb{E}[T_{\text{phase}}] \approx \frac{A\,B}{l^2\,k}\cdot m^2 k = \left(\frac{A}{l}\right)\!\left(\frac{B}{l}\right) m^2 \text{ hours}.$$

The \(k\) cancels: bigger wins come more rarely and take longer, exactly offsetting. What sets the speed is \(m/l\), i.e. the size of the position: the account's balance moves like a Brownian motion whose variance per hour is \((N\sigma)^2\), and the time to cover distances \(A\) and \(B\) is \(AB/(N\sigma)^2\). For FTMO phase 1 at \(m = 0.5\) this is \(6.67^2\times 0.25 \approx 11\) hours of open market. In practice the minimum trading days, the trading window, weekends, news and reviews dominate: phase 1 takes about four calendar days at FTMO because the rules require four trading days, not because the market is slow.

This is why the plan's risk per trade matters so much: time scales like \(1/l^2\). Doubling the risk would quarter the market time. The plan keeps 1.5% because that is what the firms' rules and stated guidance allow (Chapter 10), and gets its speed from a short stop instead, which raises the notional at the same risk.

8.3 Value per month: the renewal–reward theorem

The plan runs slots: one account position at a firm, refilled with a new evaluation the moment an attempt ends (a failed phase or a lost funded account). Over a long run a slot completes many attempts, each with value \(V_i\) and duration \(T_i\), independent and identically distributed. The average value per unit time after \(n\) attempts is \(\sum V_i / \sum T_i\), and by the law of large numbers

Renewal–reward theorem $$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n V_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n T_i} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[V]}{\mathbb{E}[T]}, \qquad \text{EV per month} = 30.44\times\frac{\mathbb{E}[V]}{\mathbb{E}[T\ \text{in days}]}.$$

The ratio of expectations, not the expectation of ratios: an attempt that lasts five months and one that lasts five days are weighted by their length. This is the objective the plan maximises for every programme.

8.4 How precise a simulated figure is

With \(n\) simulated attempts, the estimate \(\hat r = \bar V/\bar T\) has, by the delta method, standard error

$$\operatorname{se}(\hat r) \approx \frac{\operatorname{sd}(V_i - \hat r\,T_i)}{\sqrt{n}\;\bar T}.$$

Every "±" in this document is \(1.96\) of these standard errors (a 95% interval), converted to dollars per month. With 24,000 attempts it is typically ±$100–$200 a month per account.

8.5 The plan's attempt, timed

FTMO at the plan's setting. Phase 1 takes on average 5.77 trades (5.6); the first lasts 1.68 hours of open market on average (4.5), later ones a little longer (the target is farther). So the market time of a phase is a few hours, and the calendar is set by the rules: four trading days per phase, a review of one day after phase 1 and up to three after phase 2, the 14-day wait for the first payout, and the trading window (no new trades after 20:00 UTC, none at weekends).

Engine, fresh paths: an attempt lasts \(\mathbb{E}[T] = 8.68\) days on average, from the purchase to the failed phase or the lost funded account.

So one slot buys \(30.44/8.68 = 3.51\) evaluations a month and pays \(3.51\times\$632 = \$2{,}217\) a month in fees before refunds.

Value of an attempt (engine): \(\mathbb{E}[V] = \$1{,}557\); pass rate 30.7%; cash per funded account $7,124.

Renewal–reward (8.3): \(30.44 \times 1{,}557 / 8.68 = \$5{,}463\) per month, ± $342 (95%).

The median time from buying an attempt that pays to its first payout is 26 days; 7.0% of attempts reach a payout.

Decomposed (engine, 6,000 attempts on path 29): 51% of attempts fail in phase 1, and an attempt that fails anywhere in the evaluation lasts 4.1 days on average, mostly the minimum days and the wait to the next trading day. An attempt that passes spends 10.9 days in the evaluation (four trading days per phase and the reviews) and 8.1 days funded, making 0.30 payouts on average. The weighted average, \((1-P)\times4.1 + P\times(10.9 + 8.1)\), is the 8.9 days of an attempt on this path.

9.2 The search

  1. Grid. For every programme and every market it may trade: \(k \in \{2,3,4,5,6,7,8,10,12\}\), \(m \in \{0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25, 1.5\}\) (only stops the firm's leverage allows, Chapter 10), and three to five cycle targets suited to the programme's payout rules.   2. Refine. For each programme, the eight best grid settings, the version 5 default, and extensions wherever the best sat on the edge of the grid were run again for 24,000 attempts on four different paths (25–28).
  3. Edges. Wherever the refined best still sat on an edge, one more step was taken in that direction (\(k\) up to 20, stops down to the leverage limit, cycles up to 25%), again for 24,000 attempts on paths 25–28.
  4. Bold play. Because the best settings kept moving toward large \(k\), large cycles and tight stops, a last round tried \(k \in \{20, 30, 50\}\), cycles of 25%, 30%, 40% and 50%, and stops of 0.25, 0.3 and 0.35 where leverage allows. In all, 1,335 settings were refined, 32.0 million attempts.
  5. Choose, within practical limits. The plan's setting is the highest refined value per month among settings with a stop of at least 0.35 hourly moves, a cycle of at most 30% of the account and \(k\) of at most 30 (9.3 explains each limit). Where a wider stop is as good at the assumed cost and much better if costs are higher, the wider stop is used (FundedNext, 9.3).
  6. Report. The chosen setting is run a third time on four fresh paths (29–32; 33–36 for the one re-chosen for robustness) and on the real price history. Every figure reported for a programme comes from this last run, so none is inflated by having been picked as the best of many noisy estimates (the "winner's curse"). The curse is visible: the chosen settings average 10% less on fresh paths than on the paths that chose them.

9.3 What the search found, in general

Every programme moved the same way. Compared with version 5's default (\(k = 5\), \(m = 0.75\), cycles of 10%), the chosen settings have a larger \(k\) (7 to 30), the tightest stop the leverage and the limits below allow, and cycles of 20–30% of the account. Over the thirteen programmes in the plan, one 100K account each, the value rises from $47,905 to $59,847 a month (+$11,942, 25%), measured on paths that played no part in the choice.

Why. Three effects, each derived in Part I, point the same way:

  1. Cost per trade falls with a wider target (bold play, 5.6): the pass probability is \((B - \mathbb{E}[\text{cost}])/(A+B)\) exactly, and the fewest trades means the least cost. A 1 : 30 bracket reaches the target in one win; a 1 : 5 bracket needs two or three wins and more losses in between.
  2. Time falls with a tighter stop (8.2): the market time of a phase is \((A/l)(B/l)m^2\) hours, so halving \(m\) quarters it. The cost per trade rises like \(1/m\), which the large \(k\) offsets.
  3. Fewer, larger payouts (7.3): a funded account pays its allowance \(\alpha D\) on average whatever the cycle; larger cycles mean fewer trades (less cost) and fewer payout dates to wait for.

Where it stops: the plan's limits. Left free, the search keeps going toward \(k = 50\), cycles of 40–50% and stops of 0.25. The plan stops at a stop of 0.35, a cycle of 30% and \(k = 30\), for reasons the model cannot price:

  • Stop of at least 0.35 hourly moves (30 Nasdaq points, 6.6 USDJPY pips, $4.1 on gold). The cost \(\kappa\) is an average of measured spreads and commissions. Real spreads widen several-fold at the daily break, at the open and around news, and a stop a few spreads from the price is hit by the widening alone. The engine does not model this, and its effect grows as the stop tightens. For FTMO the uncapped setting (50 / 0.25 / 40%) is worth $6,560 a month on the fresh paths and $6,004 on the real history, against $5,463 and $5,971 for the plan's. The difference is real in the model and is given up deliberately.
  • Cycle of at most 30% of the account. A $40,000–$50,000 payout on a $100,000 account after one trade invites a review of the kind every firm reserves the right to make. Beyond 30% the measured gains are within the noise for most programmes.
  • \(k\) of at most 30. With cycles of at most 30% and a 1.5% risk, a win of \(k\,l\) already reaches every target at \(k \approx 27\). Larger \(k\) changes nothing but the order size on paper.

Robustness to costs. The cost check (Chapter 31) asks what each setting is worth if real costs are 1.5 or 2 times the assumption. Two accounts trade the dearer currency markets and were checked at neighbouring stops. FundingPips on USDJPY: at \(m = 0.35\) $4,991, $3,155 and $1,163 a month at 1, 1.5 and 2 times the cost; at the search's own choice \(m = 0.5\) $5,341, $4,692 and $3,510; at 0.75 $5,050, $4,130, $3,744. Chapter 10

Position size, leverage and margin

A setting is only usable if the firm lets you open the position it implies. This chapter turns the settings into lots and checks them against each firm's leverage.

10.1 From risk to lots

The stop distance in price points is \(s\times\text{price} = m\sigma\times\text{price}\). If one lot gains or loses \(V\) dollars per point (read it from the firm's contract specification), then

Lots for a given risk $$\text{lots} = \frac{l}{\text{stop points}\times V} = \frac{l}{m\,\sigma\,\text{price}\,V}, \qquad \text{notional} = \text{lots}\times\text{price}\times V = \frac{l}{m\sigma}.$$

Round lots down to the firm's lot step, so the realised risk is never above 1.5%. The take-profit in points is the stop in points times \((w + c)/l\), where \(c\) is the measured cost of the round trip in dollars for that lot size.

10.2 Leverage and the smallest usable stop

The firm holds margin of notional \(\div\) leverage. A position needs

$$\text{margin} = \frac{\text{notional}}{\lambda} = \frac{l}{m\,\sigma\,\lambda}$$

for leverage \(\lambda\) (1:\(\lambda\)). The plan allows at most 60% of the balance as margin, which leaves the margin level above 160% even after a full stop-out and keeps the account clear of the "excessive margin use" that some firms review. With \(l = 1.5\%\) of the account, this gives the smallest stop the firm allows:

10.4 The ten-minute rule

FundingPips groups every position on the same market in the same direction, including a position opened within 10 minutes of closing a losing one, into one "trade idea", and closes the account if a trade idea loses 2% (realised and unrealised together). Two stopped-out 1.5% trades in a row in the same direction within 10 minutes would therefore break it. The plan waits at least 10 minutes after any losing trade before opening the next one in the same direction, at every firm. In the simulation the next entry is always at a later hour's open, which already satisfies this.

  • a 2% stop on a 2% limit leaves no room for slippage, so the usable maximum at those firms is about 1.5–1.75% anyway.

The one large gap is FTMO 1-Step (0.468 against 0.344): its maximum loss trails the best end-of-day balance, which the chain (a static floor) does not model. That is also why the 1-Step is worth less.

Funded cash. The engine pays 4% less than the chain on average. Most of it is financing. FTMO with financing switched off is worth $5,828 a month against $5,509 with it. The daily loss limit, also left out of the chain, costs a little value and more time: in an engine experiment without it, an FTMO attempt is worth $1,600 against $1,505 with it, but $6,581 a month against $5,243, because stopping for the day after two or three losses stretches the calendar.

Conclusion. Two independent programs, one a rule-by-rule simulation on price paths and the other a set of exact equations on the trade-level chain, agree on every programme to within the effects the equations leave out, and those effects have the expected signs. Every reported value comes from the engine, the more conservative of the two.

27.2 Four 100K accounts or two 200K?

At a firm whose cap is \(\$400{,}000\), the cap can be filled with four 100K accounts, two 200K accounts, or a mix. Scale every dollar amount of a programme by a factor \(\lambda\) (account, targets, limits, risk, cycle target) and every formula of Part I scales with it: the pass probabilities (ratios of distances) are unchanged, the funded cash scales by \(\lambda\), the cost per trade scales by \(\lambda\) (it is a fraction of the notional, which scales by \(\lambda\)), and the time is unchanged (it depends on ratios of distances and on \(m\)). Only the fee may not scale. Hence

Scaling an account $$\mathbb{E}[V](\lambda S) = \lambda\left(\Pr(\text{pass})\,\mathbb{E}[C](S)\right) - F(\lambda S), \qquad \mathbb{E}[T](\lambda S) = \mathbb{E}[T](S).$$

Two 200K accounts and four 100K accounts have the same value per month if and only if the 200K fee is exactly twice the 100K fee. Where it is cheaper per dollar, 200K is better by the saving times the attempts per month; where dearer, 100K is better. Beyond the fee, five differences decide:

  1. Rules by size. Some programmes change at 200K (Hola Prime's daily and maximum loss fall to 4% and 8%) or stop at 100K (FundingPips Flex, The5ers High Stakes, FXIFY Classic, Maven).
  2. One account allowed. Where the copying rules allow only one account (FunderPro, GFT, FundedNext's independent account), the largest size the cap allows is simply worth more: twice the value per month at 200K.
  3. Payout requests. Two 200K accounts make half as many payout requests for the same money. If refusals are a risk per request (Chapter 29), fewer, larger requests lose the firm less often.
  4. Spread of outcomes. Four accounts started at different times are not perfectly correlated, so four 100K accounts give a smoother cash path than two 200K accounts with the same mean.
  5. Work and lot size. Fewer accounts mean fewer orders; larger accounts round lot sizes less.

Chapter 28

Running every account at once

28.1 Why the values add

By linearity (2.1), the expected value per month of a set of slots is the sum of their values, however the accounts are correlated. What correlation changes is the spread: in a bad week every Nasdaq account loses together.

28.2 One direction per market

No two of your accounts ever hold opposite positions on the same market at the same time. Most firms call that hedging across accounts, and FTMO forbids it across firms as well. So the plan uses one direction rule per market group: when a new trade opens and another of your accounts already holds that market, the new trade takes the same direction; when none does, the rule decides (the sign of the last five hourly closes). This rule has no edge (version 4 tested it on eight independent paths: +$280 ± $529 a month against random). Its only job is to keep every account on the same side.

28.3 The lockstep simulation

The portfolio results come from an engine that runs every account of one person on one shared price path and one calendar (lockstep.py). Trades are processed in the order of their entry time, so the shared-direction rule is enforced exactly, every firm's rules are applied to its own accounts, and every fee, phase result and payout is written to a dated ledger. Each portfolio is run for 400 simulated years: 50 independent first years of the plan on each of 8 independent sets of paths (Nasdaq paths 21–28 with their own gold, yen and euro paths). The real history is not used here, because it has no independent gold, yen and euro histories aligned with the Nasdaq; each programme's real-history check is in its own chapter. In 2,800 simulated years, no two accounts ever held opposite positions.

Chapter 29

If payouts can be refused

Every firm keeps the right to refuse a payout it judges to break its rules, so each payout is, in the end, the firm's decision. Nobody publishes how often it happens. This chapter turns that uncertainty into numbers.

29.1 A model

Suppose each payout request at a firm is refused with probability \(\rho\), independently, and that a refusal ends your use of that firm (in practice it usually comes with the account closed). The number of paid requests before the first refusal is geometric:

$$\Pr(\text{first refusal at request } j) = (1-\rho)^{j-1}\rho, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{paid requests}] = \frac{1-\rho}{\rho}.$$

If a firm's accounts make \(r\) requests a month, the firm is expected to last \(1/(\rho r)\) months, and over a horizon of \(H\) months the fraction of its all-paid value you keep is about

$$\frac{1 - e^{-\rho r H}}{\rho r H}.$$

Two consequences follow. A firm with many accounts makes many requests and is lost sooner, so stacking accounts at one firm is worth less than it looks; and spreading the same money over more firms keeps more of it. In the recommended plan the four FTMO accounts together make about 1.38 requests a month: at \(\rho = 2\%\), FTMO is expected to last \(1/(0.02 \times 1.38) = 36\) months. The single FunderPro 200K account makes 0.45 requests a month: at \(\rho = 15\%\), it is expected to last \(1/(0.15\times0.45) = 15\) months.

30.2 Placing a trade, step by step

  1. Is a trade allowed now? Entry hours (Nasdaq 01:00–20:00 UTC, USDJPY 00:00–20:00, EURUSD 07:00–20:00, gold 01:00–20:00), weekdays only, nothing new after 19:00 UTC on Friday, nothing from 15 minutes before to 5 minutes after a high-impact US release, and at least 10 minutes since this account's last losing trade in the same direction (FundingPips' trade-idea rule, applied everywhere).
  2. Direction. If any of your accounts holds this market, the same direction. Otherwise the plan's rule: long if the last completed hourly close is above the close five hours earlier, short if below. The rule has no edge (Chapter 11); its only job is that no two of your accounts are ever opposite.
  3. Risk. \(l\) = the smallest of 1.5% of the account, the room above the maximum-loss floor less 0.1% of the account, and the room left in today's daily loss less 0.2%. If that is under 0.15% of the account, stop for the day.
  4. Stop. \(m\sigma\) times the current price, from the settings card; the size is \(l\) divided by the stop in points times the dollars per point per lot, rounded down.
  5. Take-profit. The win \(w\) is the smallest of \(k\,l\), the distance to the phase target or the cycle target, and any cap the firm sets (best day, daily profit, FundingPips' 55% of the phase target). The take-profit distance is the stop distance times \((w + c)/l\), with \(c\) the measured round-trip cost of this size.
  6. Place it as one bracket order: entry, stop and take-profit together. Never move either.
  7. Log it: time, account, market, direction, size, stop, target, and the fill. The log is the evidence for any review.

30.3 Phases, payouts and refills

  • When a phase target is reached: stop. If the firm requires more trading days, place one minimal trade (the smallest lot) on each remaining day where it counts trading days; where it counts profitable days the daily target of 6.4 has already met it.
  • When a funded account reaches its cycle target: stop trading it, request the payout on the first allowed day, withdraw everything above the starting balance, and start the next cycle at the start balance.
  • When an account fails (phase or funded): buy the next evaluation of the same programme the same day. A slot is never left empty: the renewal–reward value assumes it.
  • Never let an account lapse for inactivity, never abandon a funded account, never trade an account to lose it. Account rolling is prohibited at most firms, and the plan's value does not need it.

30.4 Cash needed

Fees are paid before payouts arrive. In the simulation the running total of the recommended plan (fees out, payouts in) falls to −$59,276 at its lowest in a typical first year, and to −$136,015 in the worst 5%. Starting with that much available (or starting fewer slots and adding them as payouts arrive) keeps every slot filled. The first month alone costs about $6,181 net.

30.5 Workload

About 63 bracket orders a day across 28 accounts, each set and left, plus payout requests and evaluation purchases. The order times follow the market, so the routine is best run from a written checklist with alarms at the entry hours.

31.2 Payouts refused, accounts closed

Chapter 29 puts numbers on it: $93,711 a month if payouts are refused at 2/5/15% per request by tier, $64,499 at 5/10/30%. The plan's defence is to follow every written rule exactly and keep the evidence (the trade log), not to disguise anything. Firms most often cite "gambling", "one-sided betting", copy trading from others and hedging across accounts. The plan risks 1.5% with a stop on every trade, trades both directions, copies nobody, and never holds opposite positions. But bold play does produce large single-trade wins, and a firm may still review them. That is a real risk that no model can price.

31.3 Spread widening and tight stops

The stops are 0.35–1 hourly standard deviations: Nasdaq 29.8–85.0 points; USDJPY 9.4 pips; EURUSD 9.6 pips; gold 11.9 dollars. Spreads on CFDs widen several-fold at the daily break (about 21:00–22:00 UTC), at the weekly open and around news. A stop within a widened spread of the price can be filled without the mid price touching it. The engine's cost is an average and does not model this; it is the reason for the plan's 0.35 limit. If positions held through the daily break are stopped noticeably more often than the model's win rates (4.2) imply, close them before the break. A forced close is a fair stopping time, so it costs only the extra round trip.

31.4 Rules change

Every rule in Part II was read on 4 October 2026, and prop firms change them often: leverage, consistency rules, payout schedules, prices and per-person limits most of all. Recheck each firm's rules page before buying, and rerun the programme's optimisation (optimize_v6.py) when a rule that enters the model changes.

31.5 The model itself

  • Zero skill is an assumption in your favour only if it is true. If entries are systematically late, fills systematically worse, or the price moves against new positions (adverse selection), the edge is negative and the cost check of 31.1 is the guide: twice the cost is an extra cost of \(c/l = \kappa/(m\sigma)\) per trade: 4.7% of the risk on the Nasdaq at \(m = 0.5\), 6.7% at 0.35.
  • Volatility changes. \(\sigma\) moves over time; stops are set in units of the current \(\sigma\), so the plan adapts, and the calibration is the 2024–26 average.
  • Gaps and limits. The engine fills gaps at the open and fails accounts whose daily or maximum loss a gap breaks, as the firms do.
  • Independence of firms. Values add (linearity), but in a bad week every Nasdaq account loses together; the month table of Chapter 28 shows the spread.
  • Taxes and payment costs are not modelled.

31.6 What is not allowed, and is not in the plan

No hedging across accounts or firms; no accounts in anyone else's name; no copy-trading services or other people's signals; no trading of news windows the firm restricts; no tick scalping, latency or gap arbitrage; no grid or martingale sizing; no abandoned or deliberately failed accounts. Do not share or sell the plan: several firms treat traders who run the same marketed strategy as a group, and close them together.
