Chapter 17 · new in versions 3–4

Every trade resolved on a price path

Version 2 modelled each account with a calendar and the firm's rules, but drew each trade's outcome and duration from distributions measured once on real data. From version 3 on, every trade is opened at a real hour, its stop and target are checked bar by bar against an hourly price path, and the firm's rules (daily loss, floors, minimum days, profitable days, payout dates, caps, refunds) are applied around it. Version 4 adds a lockstep engine: all of one person's accounts run on one price path and one calendar at the same time, so the rule that no two accounts may hold opposite positions is enforced trade by trade.

17.3 Traps found on the way

Each of these made a simulation look better than reality. All are fixed in every figure of version 4, and each is a trap for anyone writing their own test.

  1. Skipping the entry bar. Checking the stop and target only from the next hour onwards gave a zero-skill 1 : 3 trade a fake +0.14 R.
  2. Checking the price too rarely. Sampling 12 times an hour misses stop touches in between: a fake +0.11 R.
  3. Overshooting the target with several positions. Profit beyond a target is wasted pass probability (Chapter 18).
  4. A take-profit equal to the distance left. The cost turns the last "win" into a loss; the target must be the distance left plus the cost.
  5. Ignoring gaps against the daily limit. A price gap can push a loss past the stop and through the daily loss limit. Version 4 fails the account when that happens.
  6. Holding where the plan says flat. The plan is flat at weekends on every account, evaluation included, and futures firms forbid overnight positions. Version 4 enforces both.
  7. Padding "profitable days" with tiny trades. The5ers counts only days with at least +0.5% profit and forbids constructing them artificially. Version 3 had padded them with tiny trades; that overstated The5ers by about a third. Version 4 uses a daily profit target with full risk on every trade (Section 26.4).
  8. Letting a big win break a best-day rule. On funded accounts with a consistency rule, the take-profit is now capped so that one day can never exceed the limit.

Chapter 18 · new in version 3

Many positions at once

The idea: instead of one trade at a time on one instrument, open several at once (Nasdaq and S&P 500, or many pairs), so the account reaches its target or floor sooner. This chapter works out what that can and cannot do, then tests every variant on price paths.

18.1 Three facts

  1. The pass chance does not change. A fair game stays fair however many bets run at once, so a phase still passes with probability B ÷ (A + B). That holds only if profit beyond the target is not wasted (18.3).
  2. Correlated positions in the same direction are one bigger bet. Two positions risking L1 and L2 on instruments with correlation ρ carry the risk of a single position of √(L12 + L22 + 2ρL1L2). Nasdaq and S&P 500 move together with ρ ≈ 0.9, so 1.5% on each is about 2.9% on one idea. That is twice FTMO's 1–1.5% guidance on what is really one trade, and FundedNext lists "concentrated directional exposure" and several trades in the same direction as prohibited one-sided betting. It also costs more than a single Nasdaq position of the same size, because the S&P CFD costs about twice as much per unit of movement (Chapter 9).
  3. Independent positions add speed, but the calendar does not move. Two independent positions double the rate at which the balance moves, so trading time halves. But an attempt also contains minimum trading days, account reviews and payout dates, which no amount of trading shortens.

18.2 Results

{{MULTI_TABLE}}

Read the table in three groups.

  • Same total risk, split across correlated instruments (Nasdaq + S&P at 0.75% each): 28% worse than one Nasdaq position at 1.5%. The risk is the same, so there is no speed gain, and the spread is paid twice.
  • Twice the risk, via a correlated second position (1.5% each): 12% better than one 1.5% position, but 11% worse than simply trading one Nasdaq position at 3%. Stacking is the expensive way to take a bigger bet, and either way the bet exceeds the guidance.
  • Independent instruments (gold, yen, euro next to the Nasdaq): worse, because every other instrument costs two to three times more per unit of movement. Even an imaginary second Nasdaq with independent prices and identical costs adds only 1–4%, within the noise, because the calendar dominates.

18.3 The overshoot trap

With several positions open, the target can be crossed by more than one of them. Profit beyond the target is pass probability thrown away: by optional stopping, P(pass) = (B + overshoot at the floor) ÷ (A + B + overshoot at the target + overshoot at the floor). Four uncapped positions (no costs) passed Phase 1 only 43% of the time, against the theoretical 49.6%, because the average balance at a pass was +$13,665 rather than +$10,000. Capping the combined target of all open positions at the distance left restored 49.9%.

Verdict

One position at a time, on the cheapest instrument per unit of movement, at the top of the firm's guidance. More positions help only if a second instrument is as cheap as the Nasdaq and independent of it, and none is. The way to grow income is more accounts, not more positions per account (Chapter 21).

Chapter 19 · new in versions 3–4

Account size, account type and the settings that matter

19.1 50K, 100K or 200K

{{SIZE_TABLE}}

The value of an account is proportional to its loss allowance, and so is everything else, including the risk per trade. What differs is the fee per dollar of allowance: FTMO charges about 0.63% of the account size for 100K and 200K, but 0.81% for 50K. So, per dollar of the $400,000 cap, two 200K accounts and four 100K accounts are worth about the same, and 50K accounts less. Four 100K accounts can be started at different times; two 200K accounts mean fewer logins and orders.

19.2 1-Step or 2-Step

FTMO's 1-Step (one 10% target, 90% split, but a 3% daily limit, an end-of-day trailing floor and a 50% best-day rule) gives {{FTMO1}} per month per slot, against {{FTMO2}} for the 2-Step. The best-day rule forces profits to be spread over several days, and the trailing floor shrinks the allowance after good days. The 2-Step is better for this plan.

19.4 Why 1 : 5

Trades per phase fall in proportion to the reward-to-risk ratio k (A × B ÷ k L2), and so does the spread paid. Time per trade rises in proportion to k, so the time per phase barely changes. The gain flattens between 1 : 5 and 1 : 8 because near a target most trades are capped at the distance left anyway; on the real 2024–26 path the best was 1 : 4 to 1 : 5. The plan uses 1 : 5.

The price of 1 : 5 is psychological. Only about one trade in six wins. A run of ten losses in a row has a probability of about (5/6)10 ≈ 16% at any given point and will happen many times a year. Nothing is wrong when it does.

19.5 Funded accounts: run cycles of the full allowance

With zero edge, the total paid out by a funded account averages the split times its loss allowance, whatever the payout policy (Chapter 5). What the policy changes is time. A small first target (+$1,000) reaches the first payout safely, but then the account sits idle waiting for the date. Aiming for +10% (+12% on a 12% allowance) in every cycle finishes accounts sooner and recycles the slot into a new attempt. At FTMO: {{X_GAIN}}.

19.6 If your costs are higher

{{COST_TABLE}}

The plan tolerates moderate cost differences between firms: half again the measured Nasdaq cost loses 4–11% of the value, double the cost 12–19%. Measure each firm's spread before you start (Section 25.3).

21.3 Why accounts move together, and what it costs

No two of your accounts may hold opposite positions at the same time; at most firms that is hedging, and FTMO also forbids it across firms. So every Nasdaq account trades the same direction at the same time, and their results move together: in a bad week every account loses at once, and funded accounts tend to pass and fail in batches. That is why the cash low points above are large and why the first months are usually negative. Expected value is unaffected by moving together; only the spread of outcomes is.

21.4 Failed accounts per firm

A zero-skill plan fails most of its accounts quickly, and every failed evaluation is replaced at once. FundedNext lists "multiple account breaches quickly" and "losses exceeding gains" among the signs of the gambling behaviour it prohibits, and FTMO monitors "account rolling". Running four accounts at one firm means about ten failed accounts a month at that firm:

One account per firm keeps every firm at about two failed accounts a month, which is what any losing trader produces. That is why Part 2 recommends one account per firm as the default and treats more as a deliberate choice.

Read this before the totals

These are expected values under three assumptions: zero skill, every firm pays every payout, and no firm changes its rules. Payouts are discretionary. Running the same short, systematic trades on many accounts at eleven firms is exactly what risk teams review. One refusal usually closes the account and bars the person. Several prop firms have failed or stopped paying since 2023. The totals are an upper limit of what the contracts are worth, not a forecast of income.

What to do with this

Pick one simple rule, write it down, apply it identically on every account, and plan on the zero-edge figures. If after several hundred trades your rule's full-size win rate at 1 : 5 is clearly above 1 in 6 after costs, that is the first evidence of an edge; until then there is none.

23.1 What could still close an account

  • Discretion. Every firm reserves the right to refuse payouts and close accounts for behaviour it judges to be gambling or not like real trading. A zero-skill plan has losses roughly equal to gains and many quick failures, which are listed signs at FundedNext. Following every written rule lowers this risk; it cannot remove it.
  • Rule changes. Several rules above changed during 2026 (FundingPips risk per trade, weekend rules; Apex's whole model). Re-read the terms before every purchase.
  • Firm failure. Payouts depend on the firm staying solvent and willing to pay.

Do not share or sell this plan

If several people trade the same written rules, their accounts produce the same trades at the same moments. FTMO calls that group or copy trading, FundedNext monitors synchronized entries across traders, Alpha Capital calls it group trading, FXIFY prohibits herd trading, and GFT closes accounts that use "any third-party strategy ... or one marketed to pass assessment accounts". A plan like this works, in so far as it works at all, only for one person trading it privately. Selling it would also mean selling expectations that depend on discretionary payouts and zero-edge maths, not on any trading skill.
