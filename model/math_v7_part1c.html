Chapter 7

What a funded account pays

On the funded account the plan trades each payout cycle to a profit target \(X\) above the starting balance, stops, requests the payout on the first allowed day, and withdraws everything above the start. The balance is back at the start, the static floor is still \(D\) below it, and the next cycle begins. If a payout date comes before the target, the plan withdraws whatever profit there is (where the firm's rules allow) and starts a new cycle; the cycle formulas below describe the simpler policy of paying only at the target, and the engine simulates the plan's. The account ends when it is abandoned near the floor under the rule of 5.4 (or, at a few firms, after a fixed number of payouts).

7.1 The withdrawal identity, with its conditions

Take one cycle. Its balance (relative to the start) is a stopped sum of trades, bounded between \(-D\) and \(X\) plus one win, so optional stopping applies to it: if cycle \(i\) ends with balance \(Z_i\) (either its target \(X_i\), withdrawn, or the abandonment level) after costs \(K_i\), then \(\mathbb{E}[Z_i] = -\mathbb{E}[K_i]\). The account's total withdrawal is \(W = \sum_i X_i\,\mathbf{1}\{\text{cycle } i \text{ paid}\}\) and it ends with balance \(X_{\text{end}}\) in its last cycle. Adding the cycles (the number of cycles has finite mean because each fails with a probability bounded away from zero):

The withdrawal identity, general form $$\mathbb{E}[W] = -\,\mathbb{E}[X_{\text{end}}] - \mathbb{E}[\text{total funded cost}].$$

Only when the account always ends exactly at the floor (\(X_\text{end} = -D\)) does this become \(\mathbb{E}[W] = D - \mathbb{E}[\text{cost}]\), the form version 6 stated for every policy. It changes when the account can end elsewhere: abandonment a little above the floor (version 7's rule: \(\mathbb{E}[-X_\text{end}]\) slightly below \(D\)), a daily-limit breach part-way down, profit forfeited (GFT's day cap, Maven's rolling cap), a closure after a fixed number of payouts (Apex), or a floor that moves (Topstep, Apex). Version 6 asserted the static identity for the trailing futures accounts; that was wrong, and those chapters now report only what the engine measures. Version 6's proof also appealed to boundedness of the balance plus withdrawals, which is not bounded; the cycle-by-cycle argument above is the correct one.

7.2 Cycle by cycle

Each cycle is a fresh run of the chain of 5.5 from 0 to its target \(X_i\) before the abandonment level, with success chance \(q_i\). Cycles continue until one fails, so cycle \(j\) is paid with probability \(\prod_{i\le j} q_i\). With a first cycle \(X_1\) and later ones \(X\) (chances \(q_1, q\)):

Funded cash, cycle form $$\mathbb{E}[C] = \alpha\left(X_1 q_1 + X\,\frac{q_1\,q}{1-q}\right) + R\cdot q_1 q^{\,r-1},$$

where the refund \(R\) comes with the \(r\)-th payout (\(r=1\) at most firms; FundingPips Standard and Blue Guardian 4, Maven 3); a refund in \(r\) equal parts with the first \(r\) payouts (Hola Prime) contributes \(\frac{R}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r} q_1 q^{\,j-1}\). With two special first cycles (GFT: the first two payouts are capped, so the first two cycles have target \(X_a\) and chance \(q_a\), later ones \(X_b\), \(q_b\)):

Two special first cycles (GFT) $$\mathbb{E}[\text{paid cycles}] = q_a + q_a^2 + \frac{q_a^2\,q_b}{1-q_b} = q_a + \frac{q_a^2}{1-q_b}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[W] = X_a\,(q_a + q_a^2) + X_b\,\frac{q_a^2\,q_b}{1-q_b}.$$

(Version 6 printed \(q_1/(1-q)\) for GFT, the one-special-cycle formula; its number came from the correct one.) In the zero-cost idealised model \(q = D/(X+D)\), \(\mathbb{E}[\text{paid cycles}] = D/X\) and \(\mathbb{E}[W] = D\), which is where the zero-cost example of 1.3 takes \(q\) from.

7.3 What the cycle target changes

In the idealised zero-cost model, nothing about the average. With costs, at fixed \(l\) and \(k\), the expected number of funded trades grows with \(X\): by 5.3 it is about \(XD/v\) per cycle times \((X+D)/X\) expected cycles, i.e. \(D(X+D)/v\). So a larger cycle means more trades and more cost over the account's life, and more time between payouts; a smaller one means more payout requests and more waiting for payout dates. Version 6 said larger cycles mean fewer trades and less cost; that contradicted this formula and is withdrawn. With caps, daily targets and payout calendars the balance between the two effects is found by search (Chapter 9), and the measured effect of \(X\) is shown in each programme's chapter.

7.4 Funded rules and what they change

  • Best-day and largest-trade rules. If no day may exceed a share \(\beta\) of a payout's profit, each day's take-profit is capped at \(\beta X\) less what the day has made (FXIFY 25%, Hola Prime 40%; the plan's own 40% everywhere). Maven requires the largest winning trade to be at most 20% of the payout's profit, so each win is capped at \(0.2X\) and a payout is requested only when the rule holds.
  • Profitable days per cycle (FundingPips 95%: 3; Hola Prime: 3; GFT: 4): the daily profit target of 6.4, applied per cycle.
  • Payout calendars. The first payout is allowed 14 to 30 days after the account opens, then every 10 to 30 days. A cycle that reaches its target early waits, flat.
  • Payout caps (GFT: the first two payouts at most 6% or $10,000): the first two cycles aim at the cap.
  • Daily profit caps (GFT: $3,000 a day at every size, excess deducted): the take-profit is capped at what is left of the day's cap.
  • Rolling caps (Maven: $10,000 per trader per rolling 30 days, excess voided): the plan requests a payout only when the window has room for all of it, and otherwise waits.

Without costs, rules that only cap or delay wins leave the average withdrawal unchanged; with costs, each extra trade costs \(c\), and rules that forfeit profit (day caps, rolling caps) change it directly. The engine includes all of them.

Chapter 8

Time, and value per month

8.1 How long one trade lasts, approximately

For a driftless arithmetic Brownian motion with variance \(\sigma^2\) per hour, \(Z_t^2 - \sigma^2 t\) is a martingale, and stopping at \(+d_t\) or \(-d_s\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] = d_s d_t/\sigma^2\). For the bracket, \(d_s = m\sigma\) and \(d_t = m\sigma(w+c)/l\):

Expected duration of a bracket (hours of open market), approximation $$\mathbb{E}[\tau] \approx m^2\,\frac{w + c}{l}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\tau \mid \text{win}] \approx \frac{m^2}{3}\Big(\big(\tfrac{w+c}{l}\big)^2 + 2\,\tfrac{w+c}{l}\Big), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\tau \mid \text{loss}] \approx \frac{m^2}{3}\Big(1 + 2\,\tfrac{w+c}{l}\Big).$$

The synthetic price is geometric: its log has drift \(-\sigma^2/2\) per hour, and the barriers sit at log distances \(-\ln(1-s)\) and \(\ln(1+u)\), not \(s\) and \(u\). For the plan's brackets (\(s\) and \(u\) a fraction of a percent to a few percent) the difference is of the order of \(s\) and \(u\) themselves, a fraction of a percent of the duration. The daily flat also cuts long trades at 20:00 UTC. So these formulas are used only to explain orders of magnitude; every duration reported in Part II is measured by the engine.

8.2 How long a phase lasts

In the idealised model the number of trades is \(AB/v\) (5.3) with \(v = l(w+c)\), and each lasts about \(m^2(w+c)/l\) hours, so the market time is about \((A/l)(B/l)\,m^2\) hours, independent of \(k\). For FTMO phase 1 at \(m = 0.75\) and \(l = \$1{,}500\): \(6.67^2 \times 0.56 \approx 25\) hours of open market. The calendar is set by the rules instead: minimum and profitable days, the concentration policy's three winning days, the daily flat, weekends, reviews and payout dates.

8.3 Value per month: the renewal–reward theorem

The plan runs slots: one account position at a firm, refilled with a new evaluation as soon as an attempt ends. A slot completes attempts with values \(V_i\) and durations \(T_i\), independent and identically distributed in the model, and

Renewal–reward theorem $$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n V_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n T_i} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[V]}{\mathbb{E}[T]}, \qquad \text{EV per month} = 30.44\times\frac{\mathbb{E}[V]}{\mathbb{E}[T\ \text{in days}]}.$$

This is a long-run rate: the average over many attempts, the ratio of expectations (not the expectation of ratios). It is the objective each programme's settings are chosen for.

8.4 How precise a simulated figure is

With \(n\) independent attempts the ratio estimate \(\hat r = \bar V/\bar T\) has, by the delta method, standard error \(\operatorname{sd}(V_i - \hat r\,T_i)/(\sqrt{n}\,\bar T)\). Attempts that start at random times on the same synthetic path share its history, so they are not fully independent. Version 7's final figures therefore use 16 independent paths per programme (1,500 attempts on each) and treat each path as a cluster:

Cluster-robust interval for a ratio $$\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat r) = \frac{G}{G-1}\,\frac{\sum_{g=1}^{G}\big(V_g - \hat r\,T_g\big)^2}{\big(\sum_g T_g\big)^2},$$

where \(V_g, T_g\) are the totals on path \(g\). With only \(G\) clusters the multiplier for a 95% interval is the Student \(t\) quantile with \(G-1\) degrees of freedom, not 1.96: 2.13 for 16 paths, 3.18 for 4. Every "±" for a programme is the larger of that cluster interval and 1.96 attempt-level standard errors. The checks of Chapter 11 use 24 independent paths per programme for the same reason: with thousands of attempts on one path, the same stretch of price history is traded dozens of times, and an attempt-level interval would be several times too narrow. Portfolio lives each run on their own new path, so they are independent and the ordinary standard error applies. These are Monte Carlo intervals under the model: they say how well the model's expectation is estimated, not how much a real year can differ from it, and they carry no allowance for wrong rules, wrong costs or a wrong model.

8.5 A first year is not the long-run rate

A new portfolio pays every fee in its first weeks and receives its first payouts a month or more later; at the end of any finite horizon some accounts are part-way through and hold value not yet paid. So the first year's average cash, the average of later months, and the long-run renewal rate are three different quantities, and nothing requires them to be equal. Version 6 said linearity made its first-year figure and its sum of long-run rates "agree"; that was wrong. Chapter [[CH_LOCK]] reports them separately: the first-year average from 12-month lives, and the long-run monthly rate and the value of the accounts still open at month 12 from 36-month lives.
